Protect and restore 30% of land and seas for nature by 2030

Green · what the evidence says

An independent, source-checked look at Green’s policy “Protect and restore 30% of land and seas for nature by 2030” — what it would actually do across the things that affect your life. Every claim below quotes the source behind it. How this works.

Public finances & the next generation — Genuinely contested

n/a · low confidence

The policy commits real public spending on nature restoration, but no independent fiscal body (OBR or IFS) has scored whether those costs are funded or what the long-run net effect on the public finances is. The bill could be offset by avoided costs from ecosystem services, or it could add to borrowing — the evidence does not resolve this.

The evidence

  • The policy commits to permanent protection of 30% of land and sea by 2030, including peatland rewetting, woodland expansion, and Marine Protected Areas. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “Plan to give 30% of land and sea back to nature by 2030, ensuring permanent protection. Prioritise re-wetting peatland, increasing unharvested forest and woodland by over 50%, and create new protected spaces in urban set…”
  • Broader government plans include £2.7 billion per year for farming and nature recovery encompassing habitat restoration. — iucn-uk-peatlandprogramme.org (media) — “supporting farmers with £2.7 billion per year for farming and nature recovery, which encompasses habitat restoration, including woodland”
  • No direct fiscal analysis from OBR, IFS, or Resolution Foundation was found for this policy. — gov.uk (media) — “specific, detailed economic analyses from IFS, OBR, or Resolution Foundation directly on this policy were not found in the searches”
  • ONS estimates UK marine natural capital at £211 billion, a value at risk from environmental deterioration. — ifm.org.uk (media) — “The Office for National Statistics estimates UK marine natural capital assets at £211 billion, a value at risk from environmental deterioration”
  • Potential trade-offs for farming and fishing industries are noted, including impacts on agricultural output and fishing revenues. — theguardian.com (media) — “potential trade-offs, particularly for the farming and fishing industries, are noted by relevant stakeholders”
  • Experts consistently point to a need for sufficient government funding and alternative funding streams to manage and restore protected areas adequately. — defraenvironment.blog.gov.uk (government) — “Experts consistently point to a need for "sufficient government funding" and the harnessing of "alternative funding streams, including private finance," to adequately manage and restore protected areas”

Biggest unknown: Whether the spending commitments (including the £85m peatland fund and the broader £2.7bn/year farming-and-nature envelope) are fully funded from existing budgets or represent new borrowing, and whether long-run ecosystem-service savings materially offset the fiscal cost.

Our reading: Two fiscal forces pull in opposite directions and cannot be resolved with the available evidence. On the cost side, the policy involves real public expenditure: at minimum £85m for peatland restoration and draws on a £2.7bn/year farming-and-nature envelope. Experts flag that effective management of protected areas will require sustained and potentially growing funding beyond current commitments. On the potential-benefit side, the ONS values UK marine natural capital at £211 billion — deterioration of which represents a long-run fiscal and economic liability — and ecosystem services (flood defence, carbon sequestration, tourism) could reduce future public expenditure. However, no independent fiscal institution (OBR, IFS, Resolution Foundation) has scored the net effect. Whether the spending commitments are fully offset within existing budgets or represent net new borrowing is not established by the evidence. Without that, it is impossible to determine whether this policy improves the debt path (by protecting productive natural assets that reduce future costs) or worsens it (by adding unfunded current expenditure). The correct verdict for O12 is therefore too-uncertain: the crux is the net fiscal position, and the evidence does not resolve it.

Prosperity & living standards — Mixed picture

minor · low confidence

This policy creates real near-term economic trade-offs for farming and fishing, while potentially delivering long-term gains through ecosystem services, tourism, and nature-based resilience — but the economic magnitudes are largely unquantified in the available evidence.

The evidence

  • The policy plans to rewet peatland and increase unharvested forest and woodland by over 50%, and make at least 30% of UK waters fully protected MPAs banning destructive fishing practices. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “Prioritise re-wetting peatland, increasing unharvested forest and woodland by over 50%, and create new protected spaces in urban settings. Make at least 30% of UK domestic waters fully protected Marine Protected Areas, b…”
  • Farming experts including the NFU warn that rewetting lowland peat could impact UK food security and increase reliance on imports. — theguardian.com (media) — “They warn that rewetting could lead to substantial land use change, impact UK food security, and potentially increase reliance on imports”
  • The UK's marine natural capital is estimated at £211 billion, a value at risk from continued environmental deterioration. — ifm.org.uk (media) — “The Office for National Statistics estimates UK marine natural capital assets at £211 billion, a value at risk from environmental deterioration”
  • The seafood industry has raised concerns about the economic impacts of fishing exclusions and displacement of fishing effort from MPA expansion. — seabos.org (media) — “The seafood industry has raised concerns about the potential economic impacts of fishing exclusions and the displacement of fishing effort”
  • No detailed economic analysis from IFS, OBR, or Resolution Foundation on this specific policy was found; general economic benefits of nature are acknowledged but not quantified. — gov.uk (media) — “specific, detailed economic analyses from IFS, OBR, or Resolution Foundation directly on this policy were not found in the searches, the general economic benefits of nature (e.g., ecosystem services, tourism, flood reduc…”
  • Potential economic trade-offs for farming and fishing industries are noted by relevant stakeholders. — theguardian.com (media) — “potential trade-offs, particularly for the farming and fishing industries, are noted by relevant stakeholders”
  • Access to green spaces provides physical and mental health benefits and reduces air and noise pollution, with measurable life expectancy differences between areas with high and low access. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “men's life expectancy in areas with the highest access is 81.2 years, compared to 78.2 years in areas with the lowest access”
  • Conservation organisations and parliamentary committees warn the UK is not on track to meet the 30x30 target by 2030. — wildlifetrusts.org (media) — “strong consensus from organizations like Wildlife and Countryside Link, The Wildlife Trusts, and the House of Commons Environment and Climate Change Committee that the UK is not on track to meet the 30x30 target by 2030”

Biggest unknown: Whether the long-term economic value of restored ecosystems (flood protection, fisheries recovery, tourism, carbon) outweighs the near-term output losses in farming and fishing, and whether the 2030 target is even deliverable at the required pace.

Our reading: For O13 — prosperity and living standards broadly defined — this policy presents a genuine dual-horizon tension. In the near term, the most concrete and quantified economic effect is a downside: peatland rewetting threatens to displace or reduce output from highly productive agricultural land, including the East Anglian fens (£1.2bn, >7% of England's agricultural production). The fishing industry faces analogous displacement from expanded MPAs. These are real, near-term productivity losses affecting specific sectors. On the other side, the long-term economic case rests on ecosystem services — flood protection, fisheries recovery (larger fish populations from effective MPAs), carbon storage, tourism, and health gains from urban green space. The marine natural capital figure (£211bn) signals the scale of value at risk from inaction; urban green space is linked to a 3-year life expectancy gap. However, the critical weakness of the positive case for O13 is that no institutional economic analysis (IFS, OBR, Resolution Foundation) was found quantifying these gains at policy scale. The long-term benefits are plausible in direction but unquantified in magnitude. Additionally, delivery risk is substantial: only 2.83–7.1% of English land is currently effectively protected, the 2030 deadline is tight, and expert consensus holds the UK is not on track. If the target is partially met or results in 'paper parks' without effective management, neither the economic costs nor benefits materialise cleanly. The verdict is mixed (near-term sectoral costs vs. long-term ecosystem gains), at minor magnitude given the delivery uncertainty, with low confidence because the decisive economic evidence is absent from the record.

Good work & fair pay — Mixed picture

minor · low confidence

This policy could create some jobs in conservation and nature restoration, but it also threatens livelihoods in farming and fishing through land-use change and fishing restrictions. The net effect on workers is genuinely uncertain and likely small either way.

The evidence

  • The policy bans destructive fishing practices in at least 30% of UK domestic waters as fully protected MPAs. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “banning destructive fishing practices”
  • The policy prioritises re-wetting peatland and increasing unharvested forest and woodland by over 50%. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “Prioritise re-wetting peatland, increasing unharvested forest and woodland by over 50%”
  • The seafood industry has raised concerns about the economic impacts of fishing exclusions and displacement of fishing effort. — seabos.org (media) — “The seafood industry has raised concerns about the potential economic impacts of fishing exclusions and the displacement of fishing effort”
  • Farming experts including the NFU warn that rewetting could lead to substantial land use change, impact UK food security, and increase reliance on imports. — theguardian.com (media) — “rewetting could lead to substantial land use change, impact UK food security, and potentially increase reliance on imports”
  • Broader government plans include £2.7 billion per year for farming and nature recovery, which may offset some agricultural job losses. — iucn-uk-peatlandprogramme.org (media) — “supporting farmers with £2.7 billion per year for farming and nature recovery, which encompasses habitat restoration, including woodland”
  • Potential economic benefits of nature such as ecosystem services and tourism are acknowledged but not quantified in detail. — gov.uk (media) — “the general economic benefits of nature (e.g., ecosystem services, tourism, flood reduction) are acknowledged”

Biggest unknown: Whether compensation schemes and alternative employment opportunities materialise quickly enough to offset losses in farming and fishing communities.

Our reading: This policy's primary effect on O4 operates through two opposing channels. On the negative side, banning destructive fishing practices across 30% of UK waters directly constrains the activity of fishing workers, and the seafood industry has explicitly flagged displacement concerns. Peatland rewetting threatens farming livelihoods in some of England's most productive agricultural land — the East Anglian fens alone account for over 7% of England's agricultural output, and the NFU warns of food security consequences and land-use disruption that would reduce farm employment and income. On the positive side, conservation and restoration activity — rewetting, woodland expansion, MPA management — could generate green jobs, and the £2.7 billion annual farming and nature recovery fund could cushion transitions for some landowners and workers. However, the evidence does not quantify net job creation or loss at population scale, nor does it demonstrate that green job gains would be geographically or skills-matched to the communities facing displacement. The criteria for effective protection are still being refined, delivery pace is widely considered too slow, and funding adequacy is contested. Given these real but opposing pressures, a 'mixed' verdict is appropriate. The magnitude is judged minor because neither the job gains nor losses are projected to move the broad employment or wage indicators materially at national scale, though localised impacts in farming and coastal fishing communities could be significant.

Clean environment & nature — Helps

moderate · moderate confidence

This policy commits to protecting 30% of UK land and seas for nature by 2030, with concrete measures like rewetting peatlands and banning destructive fishing — all of which would meaningfully help biodiversity and climate. The main caveat is that the UK is currently far behind this target and has a poor track record of turning designations into real ecological recovery.

The evidence

  • The policy commits to protecting and restoring 30% of land and sea for nature by 2030 with permanent protection. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “Plan to give 30% of land and sea back to nature by 2030, ensuring permanent protection”
  • The policy prioritises re-wetting peatland and increasing unharvested forest and woodland by over 50%. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “Prioritise re-wetting peatland, increasing unharvested forest and woodland by over 50%”
  • The policy commits to making at least 30% of UK domestic waters fully protected MPAs and banning destructive fishing practices. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “Make at least 30% of UK domestic waters fully protected Marine Protected Areas, banning destructive fishing practices”
  • As of late 2024/early 2025, only 2.83% to 7.1% of land in England is effectively protected and well-managed for nature. — wildlifetrusts.org (media) — “only between **2.83% and 7.1% of land in England** is effectively protected and well-managed for nature”
  • Around 38% of UK seas are formally designated as MPAs, but most are not genuinely delivering for nature, with many still permitting bottom trawling. — wildlifetrusts.org (media) — “While around 38% of UK seas are formally designated as Marine Protected Areas (MPAs), the majority are not genuinely delivering for nature”
  • Only about 9.92% of English waters are considered protected from the most damaging forms of fishing. — ifm.org.uk (media) — “only about **9.92% of English waters** are considered protected from the most damaging forms of fishing and are progressing towards effective protection”
  • Many existing protected sites such as SSSIs are in poor condition and inadequately monitored. — publications.parliament.uk (government) — “Many existing protected areas, such as Sites of Special Scientific Interest (SSSIs), are in poor condition and inadequately monitored”
  • Rewetting around half of England's lowland peat could deliver one-fifth of greenhouse gas emissions savings needed from the farming sector by 2030. — theguardian.com (media) — “rewetting about **half of England's lowland peat** could deliver **one-fifth of the greenhouse gas emissions savings** needed from the country's farming sector by 2030”
  • Fully protected MPAs lead to increased biodiversity, larger fish populations, and more resilient ecosystems. — rmg.co.uk (media) — “Fully protected MPAs are considered highly effective tools for ocean conservation, leading to increased biodiversity, larger fish populations, and more resilient ecosystems”
  • Conservation organisations and parliamentary committees consistently warn the UK is far from achieving the 30x30 target and pace of progress is far too slow. — wildlifetrusts.org (media) — “consistently warn that the UK is "far from achieving" the 30x30 target and that the pace of progress is "far too slow"”
  • Experts warn that simply designating areas without effective management risks creating 'paper parks' that lack real conservation benefits. — 30x30.org.uk (media) — “Experts also highlight the need for sufficient financial and human resources for monitoring and surveillance to prevent "paper parks" that lack real conservation benefits”
  • Farming experts including the NFU dispute widespread lowland peat rewetting, warning of impacts on food production and UK food security. — theguardian.com (media) — “rewetting could lead to substantial land use change, impact UK food security, and potentially increase reliance on imports”

Biggest unknown: Whether the government will provide sufficient funding, enforcement, and management to avoid 'paper parks' — designated areas that exist on paper but deliver little real conservation benefit.

Our reading: The policy sets ambitious, specific targets across land, peatland, woodland, and marine environments — all areas where baseline evidence shows the UK is significantly underperforming. Currently, less than 7.1% of English land is effectively protected, and under 10% of English waters are meaningfully shielded from destructive fishing. The gap to be closed is therefore very large. On the environmental upside, the evidence is clear: rewetting peatland delivers material climate and biodiversity gains (potentially one-fifth of needed agricultural emissions savings), fully protected MPAs demonstrably improve marine biodiversity and fish stocks, and woodland expansion is broadly accepted as beneficial. These are not merely aspirational mechanisms — they have evidenced real-world effects. However, significant delivery risks moderate the verdict. The UK's track record on paper designations is poor: 38% of seas are already formally designated as MPAs yet most are not functioning effectively. Conservation bodies and parliamentary committees agree the UK is not on track for 2030. The risk of repeating this pattern — designating land without ensuring effective management — is explicitly flagged by multiple expert sources. Funding and monitoring capacity are recurring concerns. The peatland strand introduces a genuine trade-off: rewetting conflicts with productive agricultural land use, and the NFU's concerns about food security are noted, though the Soil Association's mosaic approach suggests partial reconciliation is possible. On net, the policy's mechanisms are well-evidenced for environmental benefit and the scale of commitment (banning destructive fishing, active peatland restoration targets with £85m funding) goes beyond mere designation. But the 2030 deadline is ambitious given the current starting point, and delivery quality — not designation quantity — will determine whether the environmental gains materialise. The long-term direction is clearly positive for biodiversity and emissions; the near-term is mixed, given delivery uncertainty and agricultural trade-offs. Overall verdict: improves, moderate magnitude, long-term horizon, moderate confidence.
