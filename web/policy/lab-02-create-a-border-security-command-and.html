Create a Border Security Command and Reform the Asylum System

Labour · what the evidence says

An independent, source-checked look at Labour’s policy “Create a Border Security Command and Reform the Asylum System” — what it would actually do across the things that affect your life. Every claim below quotes the source behind it. How this works.

Public finances & the next generation — Helps

moderate · moderate confidence

This policy bundle appears to improve the public finances in the near term, mainly by ending the costly Rwanda scheme and reducing expensive hotel accommodation for asylum seekers. The main caveat is that new spending commitments — a £75m Border Security Command, a new returns unit, and a £662m UK-France deal — offset some of those savings, and the long-term fiscal path depends on whether removals and deterrence actually reduce the flow of new claimants.

The evidence

  • The policy funds the Border Security Command by ending the Rwanda scheme. — labour.org.uk (manifesto) — “funded by ending the Rwanda scheme”
  • The Rwanda scheme had already cost approximately £290 million in payments, flight charters, detention, and civil servant salaries. — theguardian.com (media) — “£290 million in payments to Rwanda, costs for chartering flights that never departed, detaining and subsequently releasing hundreds of individuals, and the salaries of over 1,000 civil servants dedicated to the scheme”
  • Cancelling the Rwanda scheme saved £220 million in scheduled future payments and up to £750 million earmarked for 2024. — youtube.com (media) — “Labour's cancellation saved £220 million in scheduled future payments to Rwanda and immediately saved up to £750 million earmarked for the scheme in 2024”
  • The Border Security Command received a £75 million investment at launch. — ibanet.org (media) — “the Home Office announcing a £75 million investment in technology, personnel, and covert capabilities to combat smuggling networks”
  • A new three-year UK-France migration agreement worth £662 million was agreed in April 2026. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “a new three-year cycle of UK funding worth £662 million agreed in April 2026”
  • Reducing asylum hotels has generated an annual saving of £500 million, with hotel numbers falling from over 400 to 210. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “210 hotels in use in September 2025, down from over 400 in summer 2023, resulting in an annual saving of £500 million”
  • Hotel accommodation cost around £158 per person per night versus £20 for dispersal accommodation. — durham.ac.uk (academic) — “Hotel accommodation averaged £158 per person per night in 2024, significantly higher than dispersal accommodation at £20 per night”
  • The initial asylum backlog fell by 58% to approximately 36,000 applications by end of March 2026. — migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk (academic) — “The initial decision asylum backlog decreased to approximately 36,000 applications by the end of March 2026, marking the lowest level since 2019 and a 58% reduction from when Labour took office”
  • The asylum appeals backlog nearly doubled to a record 80,000 in 2025, driven by more initial refusals, which may generate future tribunal and legal aid costs. — migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk (academic) — “the asylum appeals backlog nearly doubled in 2025, reaching a record 80,000 applications, driven by an increase in initial asylum refusals”

Biggest unknown: Whether the Border Security Command and returns programme actually reduce small boat arrivals and the asylum accommodation caseload at scale — if flows continue or rise, the hotel and processing savings erode and costs could return.

Our reading: The clearest fiscal signal is that ending the Rwanda scheme — which had already cost around £290 million with no removals achieved — released substantial funds and eliminated a large forward liability (up to £750 million earmarked for 2024 alone). Against this, the policy incurs new costs: a £75 million BSC investment, ongoing caseworker hiring, a 1,000-staff returns unit, and a £662 million three-year UK-France deal. However, the £500 million annual saving from halving hotel use is a concrete, realised fiscal gain that significantly outweighs the identified new spending. The mechanism is plausible and the saving is already recorded, not merely projected. The counterfactual — continuing with Rwanda and high hotel occupancy — would have been substantially more expensive in the near term. The main fiscal risk lies in the appeals pipeline: the appeals backlog nearly doubling to 80,000 implies rising tribunal, legal aid, and accommodation costs for refused claimants who cannot yet be removed, which could partially offset the hotel savings over a longer horizon. On balance, the near-term fiscal trajectory improves materially, driven by a large, evidenced saving on hotel accommodation and the elimination of a demonstrably wasteful scheme. Confidence is moderate rather than high because the appeals surge introduces a real offsetting cost whose scale is not precisely bounded in the provided evidence, and the long-term picture depends on whether deterrence and removals hold down future caseload.

Cost of living — Little effect

minor · low confidence

This policy is mainly about border security and asylum processing, not household costs. Any indirect benefit comes from freeing up government money previously spent on asylum hotels and the Rwanda scheme, but there is no direct mechanism to cut food, energy, or bills for ordinary people.

The evidence

  • Policy is funded by ending the Rwanda scheme, redirecting those resources to border enforcement. — labour.org.uk (manifesto) — “funded by ending the Rwanda scheme, to dismantle criminal gangs behind small boat crossings”
  • Cancellation of the Rwanda scheme saved £220 million in scheduled future payments and up to £750 million earmarked for 2024. — youtube.com (media) — “Labour's cancellation saved £220 million in scheduled future payments to Rwanda and immediately saved up to £750 million earmarked for the scheme in 2024”
  • Reducing asylum seekers in hotels has produced an annual saving of £500 million, with hotel numbers falling from over 400 to 210. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “resulting in an annual saving of £500 million”
  • Hotel accommodation cost around £158 per person per night, far above dispersal accommodation at £20 per night. — durham.ac.uk (academic) — “Hotel accommodation averaged £158 per person per night in 2024, significantly higher than dispersal accommodation at £20 per night”

Biggest unknown: Whether fiscal savings from reduced asylum hotel use and cancelled Rwanda scheme are recycled into household cost-of-living support, or absorbed elsewhere in public finances.

Our reading: The policy is fundamentally about asylum system management and border enforcement — not a direct intervention in household cost of living. Its relevance to O2 is indirect: by cancelling the Rwanda scheme and cutting asylum hotel use, the government frees up hundreds of millions in public spending. Those savings could, in principle, be redirected to support household budgets, but the policy text makes no such commitment. The £500 million annual hotel saving and Rwanda cancellation savings are real fiscal gains, but they go to the Exchequer generally. There is no mechanism in this policy that directly reduces food prices, energy bills, or increases take-home pay for ordinary households. The magnitude is therefore at most minor and indirect, and confidence is low because the fiscal savings' eventual use is unspecified. The verdict is negligible to minor — a small positive fiscal signal with no direct cost-of-living transmission.

Crime, justice & national security — Helps

moderate · moderate confidence

The policy has delivered measurable real-world steps toward dismantling people-smuggling networks and clearing the asylum backlog, with a new Border Security Command now operational and the initial asylum backlog cut by 58%. The main caveat is that the appeals backlog has nearly doubled, key EU database access was rejected, and whether gang-dismantling operations actually reduce crossings at scale remains unproven.

The evidence

  • A new Border Security Command will be created with hundreds of investigators and cross-border police officers to dismantle criminal gangs behind small boat crossings. — labour.org.uk (manifesto) — “Labour will create a new Border Security Command with hundreds of investigators and cross-border police officers, funded by ending the Rwanda scheme, to dismantle criminal gangs behind small boat crossings”
  • The policy seeks a new security agreement with the EU for intelligence sharing. — labour.org.uk (manifesto) — “They will seek a new security agreement with the EU for intelligence sharing”
  • A returns and enforcement unit of 1,000 staff will be set up to fast-track removals. — labour.org.uk (manifesto) — “set up a new returns and enforcement unit with 1,000 staff to fast-track removals to safe countries”
  • The Border Security Command was launched in July 2024 with £75 million invested in technology, personnel, and covert capabilities to combat smuggling networks. — ibanet.org (media) — “the Home Office announcing a £75 million investment in technology, personnel, and covert capabilities to combat smuggling networks”
  • The BSC was granted counter-terrorism style powers to disrupt people-smuggling under legislation passed in December 2025. — migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk (academic) — “The Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act, passed in December 2025, grants the BSC "counter-terrorism style powers" to disrupt people-smuggling operations and imposes tougher penalties for organised immigration cri…”
  • The initial decision asylum backlog fell to approximately 36,000 by end of March 2026, a 58% reduction from when Labour took office. — migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk (academic) — “The initial decision asylum backlog decreased to approximately 36,000 applications by the end of March 2026, marking the lowest level since 2019 and a 58% reduction from when Labour took office”
  • A UK request for access to EU databases Schengen Information System and Eurodac was rejected by European officials in May 2025, limiting intelligence-sharing capability. — theguardian.com (media) — “a UK request in May 2025 for access to the Schengen Information System (SIS) and Eurodac, the EU's centralised fingerprinting system for illegal migrants, was reportedly rejected by European officials”
  • Access to those EU databases had been anticipated to significantly aid clearing the asylum backlog. — theguardian.com (media) — “Access to these databases was anticipated to significantly aid in clearing the asylum claims and appeals backlog”
  • The asylum appeals backlog nearly doubled in 2025, reaching a record 80,000 applications, driven by an increase in initial refusals. — migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk (academic) — “the asylum appeals backlog nearly doubled in 2025, reaching a record 80,000 applications, driven by an increase in initial asylum refusals”
  • Returns of those without the right to remain have increased; in the year to June 2024, 2,418 refused asylum seekers were forcibly returned and 5,100 opted for voluntary return. — irr.org.uk (media) — “In the year to June 2024, 2,418 people whose asylum claims were refused were forcibly returned, while 5,100 opted for voluntary return”
  • A new UK-France funding agreement worth £662 million over three years was agreed in April 2026 to address unauthorised migration. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “a new three-year cycle of UK funding worth £662 million agreed in April 2026”
  • Analysts argue that a comprehensive EU asylum pact and safe legal routes are also necessary alongside enforcement to reduce crossings. — blogs.lse.ac.uk (academic) — “a comprehensive asylum pact with the EU, similar to the former Dublin Regulation, and the establishment of safe and legal routes for asylum seekers are also necessary”

Biggest unknown: Whether counter-terrorism-style disruption of smuggling networks translates into a sustained reduction in small boat crossings, or whether networks simply adapt routes and methods.

Our reading: On the safety and order indicators of O5, this policy has moved from aspiration to partial delivery within parliament. The Border Security Command is operational with statutory counter-terrorism-style powers and a £75m investment, representing a genuinely new institutional instrument targeting criminal smuggling networks — a direct O5 threat. The 58% reduction in the initial asylum backlog and the rise in enforced and voluntary returns are measurable improvements to the functioning of the justice and enforcement system. The UK-France bilateral funding deal further strengthens border cooperation. These are real delivered mechanisms, not aspirational language, which clears the 'soft-verb' threshold for a positive direction. However, the magnitude is constrained by two significant problems. First, the EU rejected the UK's request for SIS and Eurodac database access — a setback that limits the intelligence-sharing ambition materially and was expected to be a key tool in clearing the backlog. Second, the appeals backlog has nearly doubled to a record 80,000, largely because faster initial refusals fed downstream into the tribunal system. This means the justice system as a whole is not yet functioning better — one stage improved, another worsened. The returns and enforcement unit represents a stated commitment with 300 staff already redeployed as a concrete first step, but the 1,000-staff target and its effectiveness at scale remain unverified. On balance, the policy has made genuine, evidenced progress on disrupting criminal networks and reducing the initial backlog and hotel use, but the appeals crisis, the EU database rejection, and unproven gang-dismantling effectiveness at scale prevent a 'major' rating. 'Moderate' improvement to O5 over this parliament, at moderate confidence.

Equal treatment & democratic rights — Mixed picture

moderate · moderate confidence

The policy speeds up initial asylum decisions, which helps people get a fair hearing faster, but new rules giving refugees shorter protection and a 20-year path to permanent residency create lasting insecurity for a vulnerable group — and the appeals backlog has nearly doubled. The net effect on equal treatment and due process pulls in opposite directions.

The evidence

  • The policy commits to clearing the asylum backlog by hiring additional caseworkers and setting up a returns unit with 1,000 staff to fast-track removals. — labour.org.uk (manifesto) — “clear the asylum backlog by hiring additional caseworkers to end the use of asylum hotels, set up a new returns and enforcement unit with 1,000 staff to fast-track removals to safe countries”
  • The initial-decision asylum backlog fell 58% from Labour taking office to approximately 36,000 by March 2026, the lowest since 2019. — migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk (academic) — “The initial decision asylum backlog decreased to approximately 36,000 applications by the end of March 2026, marking the lowest level since 2019 and a 58% reduction from when Labour took office”
  • However, the asylum appeals backlog nearly doubled in 2025, reaching a record 80,000 applications, driven by an increase in initial refusals. — migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk (academic) — “the asylum appeals backlog nearly doubled in 2025, reaching a record 80,000 applications, driven by an increase in initial asylum refusals”
  • Under legislation passed in December 2025, refugee status is now granted for only 30 months instead of five years, with a default 20-year qualifying period for permanent residence. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “refugee status is granted for 30 months instead of five years, requiring reviews for ongoing protection needs, with a default 20-year qualifying period for permanent residence”
  • Critics including Human Rights Watch argue the shortened refugee status creates 'permanent insecurity' for refugees and undermines the 1951 Refugee Convention. — hrw.org (media) — “Critics, including Human Rights Watch and academics from GRAMNet, argue that this creates "permanent insecurity" for refugees, is "cruelty," and undermines the 1951 Refugee Convention”
  • Comparable policy in Denmark produced few actual reassessments or returns but significant uncertainty for those affected. — opendemocracy.net (media) — “Denmark's similar policy where few reassessments or returns occurred but significant uncertainty was created”
  • The Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act granted the Border Security Command counter-terrorism style powers to disrupt people-smuggling. — migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk (academic) — “grants the BSC "counter-terrorism style powers" to disrupt people-smuggling operations and imposes tougher penalties for organised immigration crime”
  • Some safe routes for Afghans who assisted UK troops have been closed, potentially contradicting humanitarian commitments. — hrw.org (media) — “the government has also implemented restrictions, such as closing some safe routes for Afghans who assisted UK troops”

Biggest unknown: Whether the near-doubling of the appeals backlog proves temporary or entrenched will determine whether the overall due-process picture improves or worsens for asylum seekers.

Our reading: This policy has genuine and substantial effects in both directions on O9's core indicators — due process and minority protections — making a 'mixed' verdict the honest call. On the positive side, the 58% reduction in the initial-decision backlog is a real, measurable improvement in due process: asylum seekers get a substantive decision faster, reducing the limbo that denies them legal certainty. Faster decisions are a meaningful procedural-fairness gain. On the negative side, two developments pull strongly against O9. First, the appeals backlog has nearly doubled to a record 80,000. Since refusal rates have risen, more people are pushed into an appeals queue that has grown far faster than the initial queue has shrunk — net due-process access for refused claimants has worsened. Second, the shift to 30-month refugee status with a 20-year path to permanent residence structurally reduces the legal security of a recognised minority group. This is not a fringe critique: Human Rights Watch and academic bodies contend it creates 'permanent insecurity' and conflicts with the 1951 Refugee Convention — a foundational equal-treatment instrument. The Denmark comparison suggests the practical effect is not mass returns but prolonged legal precarity, which is itself an equal-treatment harm. Closing safe routes for some Afghans who assisted UK forces adds a further targeted reduction in access to protection for a specific group. Absent this policy, the initial backlog would likely have remained very high (the 2023 baseline was over 85,000), so that gain is plausibly additional. But the appeals surge and the refugee-status changes are also directly attributable to the policy package. The two effects are both real, evidenced, and substantial — hence 'mixed'. Neither cancels the other cleanly.

Immigration & border control — Moves toward more control

We don’t call this better or worse — that’s your call; we only show which way the policy moves it.

moderate · moderate confidence

This policy moves border and asylum rules in a more controlled direction by strengthening enforcement, speeding up removals, and reducing the asylum backlog — though how much it lowers net migration depends on whether enforcement measures work as intended.

The evidence

  • The Border Security Command was launched in July 2024 with a £75 million investment in technology and personnel. — ibanet.org (media) — “Border Security Command, headed by former National Police Chiefs' Council chair Martin Hewitt, was launched in July 2024, with the Home Office announcing a £75 million investment in technology, personnel, and covert capa…”
  • The BSC was granted counter-terrorism style powers under legislation passed in December 2025. — migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk (academic) — “The Border Security, Asylum and Immigration Act, passed in December 2025, grants the BSC "counter-terrorism style powers" to disrupt people-smuggling operations and imposes tougher penalties for organised immigration cri…”
  • The initial asylum backlog fell 58% to approximately 36,000 by March 2026, the lowest since 2019. — migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk (academic) — “The initial decision asylum backlog decreased to approximately 36,000 applications by the end of March 2026, marking the lowest level since 2019 and a 58% reduction from when Labour took office”
  • However, the asylum appeals backlog nearly doubled in 2025, reaching a record 80,000. — migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk (academic) — “the asylum appeals backlog nearly doubled in 2025, reaching a record 80,000 applications, driven by an increase in initial asylum refusals”
  • Returns of refused asylum seekers have increased, with 300 staff redeployed to enhance enforced and voluntary returns. — irr.org.uk (media) — “The government announced in August 2024 that 300 staff would be redeployed to enhance both enforced and voluntary returns”
  • A UK request for access to EU databases Schengen Information System and Eurodac was rejected by European officials in May 2025. — theguardian.com (media) — “a UK request in May 2025 for access to the Schengen Information System (SIS) and Eurodac, the EU's centralised fingerprinting system for illegal migrants, was reportedly rejected by European officials”
  • Access to those EU databases was anticipated to significantly aid clearing the asylum backlog. — theguardian.com (media) — “Access to these databases was anticipated to significantly aid in clearing the asylum claims and appeals backlog”
  • Plans to scale up removals and tighten refugee status conditions (30-month grants, 20-year path to settlement) signal further movement toward control, but critics argue this creates insecurity without reducing arrivals. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “refugee status is granted for 30 months instead of five years, requiring reviews for ongoing protection needs, with a default 20-year qualifying period for permanent residence”

Biggest unknown: Whether the Border Security Command and returns unit will achieve sufficient operational scale, and whether EU cooperation (notably SIS/Eurodac access, which was rejected in May 2025) materialises enough to make enforcement effective.

Our reading: The policy bundles stronger enforcement (Border Security Command with counter-terrorism powers, a 1,000-strong returns unit), faster asylum processing that has measurably cut the initial backlog, and tighter status conditions for refugees. All of these levers point in the same direction: more controlled borders and a lower intended stock of people in the asylum system. The return rate has risen and hotel use has fallen. The main complications are the appeals backlog doubling (offsetting some of the backlog gains) and the rejection of UK access to EU databases (limiting intelligence-sharing ambitions). On balance the direction is clearly toward more controlled, at moderate magnitude, with moderate confidence because several enforcement measures are still scaling up and diplomatic channels remain incomplete.
