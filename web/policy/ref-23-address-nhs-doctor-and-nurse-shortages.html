Address NHS doctor and nurse shortages

Reform UK · what the evidence says

An independent, source-checked look at Reform UK’s policy “Address NHS doctor and nurse shortages” — what it would actually do across the things that affect your life. Every claim below quotes the source behind it. How this works.

Tax & the money you keep — Helps

moderate · moderate confidence

Frontline NHS and social care staff would pay no basic rate income tax for three years, meaningfully boosting their take-home pay — the main caveat is that only NHS frontline workers benefit, leaving all other workers unaffected.

The evidence

  • All frontline NHS and social care staff would pay zero basic rate income tax for three years. — reformparty.uk (manifesto) — “making all frontline staff pay zero basic rate tax for three years”
  • Student fees for doctors, nurses and medical staff would be written off pro rata over 10 years of NHS service. — reformparty.uk (manifesto) — “writing off student fees pro rata over 10 years of NHS service”
  • NHS hospitals and community health services in England employed approximately 1.38 million FTE staff, including 153,600 doctors and 367,300 nurses. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “NHS hospitals and community health services in England employed 1.38 million full-time equivalent (FTE) staff, including 153,600 FTE doctors and 367,300 FTE nurses”
  • An individual frontline worker at the top of the basic rate band could save around £7,500 over the three-year period. — reddit.com (media) — “individuals maxing out the basic rate band could save around £7,500 over three years”
  • The average outstanding student debt for nurses is around £48,000. — nuffieldtrust.org.uk (institutional) — “The average outstanding student debt for nurses is around £48,000”
  • The Nuffield Trust estimates a loan-forgiveness scheme would cost approximately £230 million per year for nursing and allied health professionals, and an additional £170 million per year if extended to doctors. — nuffieldtrust.org.uk (institutional) — “The Nuffield Trust estimates such a scheme would cost approximately £230 million per year for nursing, midwifery, and allied health professionals, and an additional £170 million per year if extended to doctors”

Biggest unknown: Whether the fiscal cost forces offsetting tax rises or spending cuts that reduce take-home pay for other workers (scored on O12, not here), and whether the benefit is sustained beyond three years.

Our reading: The policy has two elements that bear on O11. First, the zero basic-rate-tax measure is a direct, quantified reduction in the tax burden for approximately 1.38 million FTE frontline NHS and social care workers. The projected saving of up to £7,500 over three years per worker is real and material to those households; with over a million workers affected this is a population-scale effect within that group. This clearly improves take-home pay for the targeted workers during the three-year window. Second, the pro-rata student debt write-off reduces the effective cost of NHS employment for participating staff — reducing an effective deduction against lifetime earnings (average nurse debt ~£48,000), which also improves net financial position over a 10-year service period. However, the benefit is narrowly targeted: only NHS and social care frontline workers gain. The rest of the working population is unaffected, so the aggregate distributional footprint is concentrated rather than broad. The time horizon is 'this-parliament' for the tax element (three years is the stated duration) and longer for the debt write-off, but the dominant and most certain effect is the near-term tax saving. Magnitude is moderate: material and population-scale within the targeted group (over a million workers), but limited to that group. The fiscal counterpart — whether offsetting tax rises or borrowing follow — is an O12 question and is explicitly excluded from this verdict. Confidence is moderate because the per-worker estimate (E9) comes from a non-institutional source (reddit), though the mechanism is arithmetically straightforward; the institutional evidence (Nuffield Trust) supports the debt-write-off element.

Public finances & the next generation — Hurts

major · moderate confidence

The policy creates large unfunded costs — zero income tax for 1.38 million frontline staff, plus loan write-offs and uncapped medical places — with no stated funding source, meaning it would likely need to be paid for by borrowing or cuts elsewhere. The IFS has explicitly flagged that Reform's tax proposals cost far more than estimated and would require offsetting measures.

The evidence

  • The policy commits to zero basic rate tax for all frontline NHS and social care staff for three years, ending training caps for UK medical students, and writing off student fees pro rata over 10 years of NHS service. — reformparty.uk (manifesto) — “making all frontline staff pay zero basic rate tax for three years, ending training caps for UK medical students, and writing off student fees pro rata over 10 years of NHS service”
  • NHS hospitals and community health services in England employ 1.38 million FTE staff, meaning the tax-free measure applies to a very large workforce. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “NHS hospitals and community health services in England employed 1.38 million full-time equivalent (FTE) staff”
  • Individuals at the top of the basic rate band could save around £7,500 over the three-year period, implying a large aggregate Exchequer cost across 1.38 million staff. — reddit.com (media) — “individuals maxing out the basic rate band could save around £7,500 over three years”
  • The IFS assessed that Reform UK's proposed tax cuts would cost 'tens of billions of pounds a year more' than the party estimated. — ifs.org.uk (institutional) — “Reform UK's proposed tax cuts would cost "tens of billions of pounds a year more" than estimated”
  • The IFS stated that these tax cuts would likely need to be offset by cuts elsewhere or increased borrowing. — uk.finance.yahoo.com (media) — “tax cuts would likely need to be offset by cuts elsewhere or increased borrowing”
  • The Nuffield Trust estimates loan forgiveness for nursing, midwifery, and allied health professionals would cost approximately £230 million per year, with an additional £170 million per year if extended to doctors. — nuffieldtrust.org.uk (institutional) — “such a scheme would cost approximately £230 million per year for nursing, midwifery, and allied health professionals, and an additional £170 million per year if extended to doctors”
  • Increasing medical student numbers by 5,000 (a managed expansion scenario) would cost approximately £1 billion annually; uncapping entirely would cost more. — ucas.com (media) — “increasing the number of medical students by 5,000 would cost approximately £1 billion annually”
  • Each medical or dental school place is estimated by government to cost around £230,000, illustrating the scale of fiscal commitment from uncapped expansion. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “the government has estimated the 'public' cost for each medical or dental school place to be around £230,000”

Biggest unknown: Whether any offsetting revenue or spending reductions would actually be delivered to fund the tax-free period, which dwarfs the other two measures in cost.

Our reading: This policy has three distinct fiscal components, all of which generate Exchequer costs with no stated funding mechanism in the policy text. The dominant cost is the zero basic-rate-tax period for frontline staff. Applied to 1.38 million FTE employees, with individual savings of up to £7,500 over three years, the aggregate cost runs into several billion pounds annually. The IFS has explicitly assessed that Reform's broader tax proposals cost 'tens of billions more than estimated' and would require offsetting cuts or additional borrowing. No offset is identified in this policy. The loan write-off scheme is better evidenced in cost terms: the Nuffield Trust puts it at roughly £400 million per year (nursing/AHP plus doctors). While the Nuffield Trust notes this is less than Treasury savings from recent student loan changes, this does not make it self-financing — it is still a new Exchequer liability unless explicitly matched to a saving. Ending training caps adds further costs: a managed 5,000-place expansion alone costs ~£1 billion annually; a full uncapping would exceed this. Critically, the BMA and Medical Schools Council warn that uncapped expansion without matching placement capacity and educator numbers risks producing unplaceable graduates — a deadweight fiscal cost with no workforce gain. In aggregate, the three measures represent several billion pounds of annual unfunded spending or forgone revenue. On O12's criteria — is spending funded or borrowed, and does it worsen the debt path — the verdict is clearly 'worsens'. The only countervailing argument would be that retaining or attracting NHS staff reduces agency/locum spend and long-run health costs, but no evidence unit provides a costed estimate of these savings sufficient to offset the fiscal gap, so this remains an ungrounded claim under the evidence-binding rules. The IFS assessment anchors the verdict firmly on the worsening side.

Healthcare — Helps

moderate · moderate confidence

This policy targets the real NHS staffing shortage through tax incentives, fee write-offs, and lifting training caps — the fee write-off has credible institutional backing, but training cap removal risks being counterproductive without matching placement capacity, and the fiscal cost of the tax exemption is disputed. Full benefits would take many years to materialise.

The evidence

  • Policy proposes zero basic rate tax for three years for all frontline NHS and social care staff, ending training caps for UK medical students, and writing off student fees pro rata over 10 years of NHS service. — reformparty.uk (manifesto) — “making all frontline staff pay zero basic rate tax for three years, ending training caps for UK medical students, and writing off student fees pro rata over 10 years of NHS service”
  • The stated aim is to retain existing staff and attract former staff back to the NHS and social care. — bmj.com (media) — “Reform UK states this policy aims to retain existing staff and attract former staff back to the NHS and social care”
  • The NHS workforce supply-demand gap was approximately 103,000 FTE in 2021/22, projected to rise to 179,000 FTE by 2024/25 and remain substantial at 156,000 FTE in 2030/31. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “the overall workforce supply-demand gap was approximately 103,000 FTE in 2021/22, projected to rise to 179,000 FTE by 2024/25 and remain substantial at 156,000 FTE in 2030/31”
  • NHS vacancies in England reached 132,139 in June 2022, a 9.7% vacancy rate. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “Vacancies in the NHS in England reached 132,139 in June 2022, a 9.7% vacancy rate”
  • The average outstanding student debt for nurses is around £48,000. — nuffieldtrust.org.uk (institutional) — “The average outstanding student debt for nurses is around £48,000”
  • Medical school places have traditionally been capped to ensure teaching standards and clinical placement availability. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “Medical school places in the UK have traditionally been capped to ensure the maintenance of teaching standards and the availability of high-quality clinical placements”
  • In 2026, over 40,000 applicants competed for approximately 11,000 specialty training posts, and 52% of FY2 doctors in England had not secured employment. — bma.org.uk (media) — “In 2026, over 40,000 applicants competed for approximately 11,000 posts, and a BMA survey in August 2025 found 52% of FY2 doctors in England had not secured employment”
  • The Nuffield Trust supports a loans forgiveness scheme, suggesting it could increase applications to clinical education courses, reduce attrition during training, and boost NHS and social care participation. — nuffieldtrust.org.uk (institutional) — “Analysis by the Nuffield Trust supports a "loans forgiveness" scheme, suggesting it could increase applications to clinical education courses, reduce attrition during training, and boost participation in NHS and social c…”
  • A nurse loans forgiveness scheme would cost approximately £230 million per year, plus £170 million if extended to doctors. — nuffieldtrust.org.uk (institutional) — “The Nuffield Trust estimates such a scheme would cost approximately £230 million per year for nursing, midwifery, and allied health professionals, and an additional £170 million per year if extended to doctors”
  • The IFS has stated that Reform UK's proposed tax cuts would cost tens of billions of pounds more than estimated, and proposed NHS spending increases would be insufficient to eliminate waiting lists within two years. — ifs.org.uk (institutional) — “Reform UK's proposed tax cuts would cost "tens of billions of pounds a year more" than estimated, and their proposed spending increases for the NHS (£17 billion per year) would be "insufficient" to meet ambitious goals l…”
  • The BMA and Medical Schools Council argue that simply ending training caps risks compromising educational quality and creating a pool of unplaceable graduates without addressing clinical placement capacity. — bma.org.uk (media) — “expansion must be carefully planned and adequately resourced in terms of clinical placements and educator numbers to avoid compromising educational quality and creating a pool of unplaceable graduates”
  • The number of people living with major illness is projected to increase by 2.6 million to 9.3 million by 2040, further intensifying demand for healthcare staff. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “the number of people living with major illness will increase by 2.6 million to 9.3 million by 2040, further intensifying demand for healthcare staff”

Biggest unknown: Whether the fiscal cost of the zero-tax exemption can be sustained without offsetting cuts that harm NHS funding, given IFS warnings that the overall fiscal package costs far more than claimed.

Our reading: The NHS faces a severe and worsening staffing gap — projected to grow from over 100,000 FTE vacancies toward 179,000 — alongside rising demand from a sicker population projected to reach 9.3 million with major illness by 2040. This policy directly targets that gap with three levers. The fee write-off is the most evidence-supported measure. The Nuffield Trust explicitly backs a loans forgiveness approach as likely to increase applications, reduce attrition, and boost NHS participation. With average nurse debt at £48,000, the financial incentive is material. Estimated cost is around £400 million per year for nursing and doctors combined — significant but bounded. The tax exemption could aid retention and re-recruitment of lapsed staff by boosting take-home pay. However, the IFS warns the overall Reform fiscal package costs tens of billions more than stated, raising a real risk that this exemption displaces rather than supplements NHS funding. If offset by cuts elsewhere, the net effect on healthcare capacity could be neutral or negative. Ending training caps is the most double-edged measure. The current bottleneck is not applicant numbers — over 40,000 competed for ~11,000 specialty posts in 2026, with 52% of junior doctors unable to secure employment. Simply uncapping undergraduate places without expanding clinical placements and training posts could worsen graduate unemployment without adding delivered NHS capacity. The BMA and Medical Schools Council explicitly warn of this risk. Benefits from increased medical graduates would also take 10–12 years to materialise. On balance, the policy direction addresses a real and severe problem, and the loans forgiveness element has credible institutional backing. But fiscal sustainability of the tax exemption is unresolved, and the training cap removal risks being counterproductive without matching post-graduate capacity. The net effect is likely a moderate improvement in healthcare access over the long term, contingent on whether the fiscal arithmetic holds.

Good work & fair pay — Helps

moderate · moderate confidence

This policy would give NHS frontline staff a significant pay boost through zero basic rate tax and fee write-offs, likely helping retain workers and attract others back — but the training cap removal could backfire by creating more graduates than there are jobs, hurting early-career doctors.

The evidence

  • All frontline NHS and social care staff would pay zero basic rate tax for three years. — reformparty.uk (manifesto) — “making all frontline staff pay zero basic rate tax for three years”
  • Training caps for UK medical students would be ended. — reformparty.uk (manifesto) — “ending training caps for UK medical students”
  • Student fees would be written off pro rata over 10 years of NHS service. — reformparty.uk (manifesto) — “writing off student fees pro rata over 10 years of NHS service”
  • The policy aims to retain existing staff and attract former staff back. — bmj.com (media) — “Reform UK states this policy aims to retain existing staff and attract former staff back to the NHS and social care”
  • The NHS workforce supply-demand gap was around 103,000 FTE in 2021/22 and projected to reach 179,000 FTE by 2024/25. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “the overall workforce supply-demand gap was approximately 103,000 FTE in 2021/22, projected to rise to 179,000 FTE by 2024/25”
  • The average outstanding student debt for nurses is around £48,000. — nuffieldtrust.org.uk (institutional) — “The average outstanding student debt for nurses is around £48,000”
  • 6,325 fewer UK nurses joined NHS hospital and community services in the year to March 2022 compared to the two preceding years, a 32% fall. — nuffieldtrust.org.uk (institutional) — “6,325 fewer UK nurses joined NHS hospital and community services in the year to March 2022 compared to the two preceding years (a 32% fall)”
  • The zero basic-rate tax benefit could save individuals around £7,500 over three years, providing a material financial incentive. — reddit.com (media) — “individuals maxing out the basic rate band could save around £7,500 over three years”
  • A loan forgiveness scheme is estimated to cost approximately £230 million per year for nursing and allied health staff, plus £170 million if extended to doctors. — nuffieldtrust.org.uk (institutional) — “the Nuffield Trust estimates such a scheme would cost approximately £230 million per year for nursing, midwifery, and allied health professionals, and an additional £170 million per year if extended to doctors”
  • The Nuffield Trust's analysis supports loans forgiveness schemes as likely to increase applications, reduce attrition, and boost NHS participation. — nuffieldtrust.org.uk (institutional) — “Analysis by the Nuffield Trust supports a "loans forgiveness" scheme, suggesting it could increase applications to clinical education courses, reduce attrition during training, and boost participation in NHS and social c…”
  • The BMA warns that expanding medical student numbers without matching specialty training posts risks career uncertainty and high debt for graduates; in 2026, over 40,000 applicants competed for approximately 11,000 posts, and 52% of FY2 doctors had not secured employment. — bma.org.uk (media) — “In 2026, over 40,000 applicants competed for approximately 11,000 posts, and a BMA survey in August 2025 found 52% of FY2 doctors in England had not secured employment”
  • Experts stress that expansion of medical places must be matched by clinical placement capacity and educator numbers to avoid compromising quality. — bma.org.uk (media) — “expansion must be carefully planned and adequately resourced in terms of clinical placements and educator numbers to avoid compromising educational quality and creating a pool of unplaceable graduates”
  • The IFS concluded that Reform UK's proposed tax cuts would cost tens of billions more than estimated and that NHS spending increases would be insufficient for stated goals. — ifs.org.uk (institutional) — “Reform UK's proposed tax cuts would cost "tens of billions of pounds a year more" than estimated, and their proposed spending increases for the NHS (£17 billion per year) would be "insufficient" to meet ambitious goals”

Biggest unknown: Whether removing training caps without expanding clinical placement capacity will produce more employable doctors or simply more indebted graduates unable to secure specialty training posts.

Our reading: The policy has three distinct levers affecting O4. First, zero basic-rate tax for three years delivers a direct, immediate pay uplift — worth roughly £7,500 over the period — to all frontline staff. Given a documented workforce gap of at least 103,000 FTE and a sharp recent fall in nurse joiners, financial incentives targeted at retention and re-entry are a plausible remedy. The Nuffield Trust independently endorses loan-forgiveness schemes as likely to increase applications and reduce attrition, supporting that element too. These two measures are likely to improve real pay and job security for existing and returning NHS workers in the near to medium term. Second, ending training caps carries a meaningful downside risk for early-career workers. The BMA documents a crisis-level mismatch already: 40,000+ applicants for ~11,000 specialty posts, with 52% of junior doctors failing to secure employment in 2025. Simply uncapping student entry without expanding placement capacity and training posts risks producing more graduates burdened by debt who cannot find NHS employment — worsening, not improving, their work security and earnings. The Medical Schools Council itself says placement capacity must be addressed first. Third, the IFS flags that the fiscal cost of the tax cuts is likely understated by 'tens of billions,' creating delivery risk: if the policy cannot be funded, none of the pay improvements materialise. On balance, the retention-and-pay elements are well-supported by evidence and would improve O4 for the existing workforce; the training cap removal, as designed, risks harming early-career workers. The net verdict is a moderate improvement, given the stronger evidence base for the pay/retention measures, but confidence is moderated by fiscal uncertainty and the training-cap risk.

Security in later life — Helps

minor · low confidence

By offering tax relief to social care frontline staff and writing off student debt for NHS workers, this policy could ease staff retention pressures that affect care for older people — but gains are contingent on fiscal sustainability and the training pipeline takes over a decade to deliver.

The evidence

  • The policy offers zero basic-rate tax for three years to all frontline staff, including social care, and writes off student fees pro rata over 10 years of NHS service. — reformparty.uk (manifesto) — “making all frontline staff pay zero basic rate tax for three years, ending training caps for UK medical students, and writing off student fees pro rata over 10 years of NHS service”
  • The policy's stated aim is to retain existing staff and attract former staff back to the NHS and social care. — bmj.com (media) — “Reform UK states this policy aims to retain existing staff and attract former staff back to the NHS and social care”
  • The overall NHS workforce supply-demand gap was projected to rise to 179,000 FTE by 2024/25 and remain substantial at 156,000 FTE in 2030/31. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “workforce supply-demand gap was approximately 103,000 FTE in 2021/22, projected to rise to 179,000 FTE by 2024/25 and remain substantial at 156,000 FTE in 2030/31”
  • Demand for healthcare staff is projected to intensify as the number of people living with major illness will increase by 2.6 million to 9.3 million by 2040. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “the number of people living with major illness will increase by 2.6 million to 9.3 million by 2040, further intensifying demand for healthcare staff”
  • The average outstanding student debt for nurses is around £48,000, indicating a real financial burden on the workforce the write-off targets. — nuffieldtrust.org.uk (institutional) — “The average outstanding student debt for nurses is around £48,000”
  • Nuffield Trust analysis supports a loan forgiveness scheme, suggesting it could increase applications to clinical education courses, reduce attrition during training, and boost participation in NHS and social care. — nuffieldtrust.org.uk (institutional) — “Analysis by the Nuffield Trust supports a "loans forgiveness" scheme, suggesting it could increase applications to clinical education courses, reduce attrition during training, and boost participation in NHS and social c…”
  • The IFS assessed that Reform UK's proposed tax cuts would cost tens of billions of pounds a year more than estimated, raising questions about fiscal sustainability of the tax-relief element. — ifs.org.uk (institutional) — “Reform UK's proposed tax cuts would cost "tens of billions of pounds a year more" than estimated”
  • Simply ending training caps without addressing placement capacity risks producing unplaceable graduates — over 40,000 applicants competed for approximately 11,000 posts in 2026, and 52% of FY2 doctors in England had not secured employment in a 2025 BMA survey. — bma.org.uk (media) — “over 40,000 applicants competed for approximately 11,000 posts, and a BMA survey in August 2025 found 52% of FY2 doctors in England had not secured employment”
  • Medical training takes 10-12 years for a GP, meaning any workforce gains from ending training caps will not materialise for many years. — medschools.ac.uk (academic) — “acknowledging the long training period (10-12 years for a GP, longer for consultants)”

Biggest unknown: Whether the zero basic-rate tax relief for all frontline staff is fiscally sustainable, and whether social care specifically retains enough staff to materially improve care access and quality for older people.

Our reading: O8 concerns dignified retirement and care in old age, which depends on an adequate social care and NHS workforce. This policy touches O8 through two channels. First, the three-year zero basic-rate tax relief covers all frontline staff — including social care workers — potentially improving retention among those who directly care for older people. This is a near-term lever with a plausible mechanism, though the IFS flags the tax cut element as dramatically undercosted, making sustained delivery uncertain. Second, the student-fee write-off for NHS service addresses a real financial burden (average nurse debt ~£48,000) and Nuffield Trust evidence specifically endorses loan forgiveness as likely to reduce attrition and boost recruitment — a credible evidential basis for a modest improvement. However, the write-off is tied to NHS service only, not social care, so its direct benefit to social care workers who support older people is limited to the tax relief alone. The training-cap removal contributes nothing to O8 in the short or medium term: the pipeline takes over a decade, and BMA data shows a severe placement bottleneck already producing unplaceable graduates. This element could worsen training quality without structural investment and is unlikely to deliver workforce gains relevant to older people's care within any realistic parliamentary timeframe. Overall the policy points in the right direction for O8 — easing some financial pressures on the workforce that cares for older people — but the effect is modest, long-term, fiscally uncertain, and not specifically targeted at social care adequacy. The verdict is improves/minor/long-term/low-confidence.
