National Food Strategy

Liberal Democrat · what the evidence says

An independent, source-checked look at Liberal Democrat’s policy “National Food Strategy” — what it would actually do across the things that affect your life. Every claim below quotes the source behind it. How this works.

Inequality & fair shares — Genuinely contested

n/a · low confidence

The policy promises to introduce a National Food Strategy with goals like ending food poverty — which would disproportionately help lower-income households and narrow inequality — but it commits to no specific instruments, budgets, or targets, and the evidence from prior UK food strategy efforts shows the gap between stated ambition and delivered action is large.

The evidence

  • The policy commits to introducing a National Food Strategy with goals including ending food poverty and tackling rising food prices. — libdems.org.uk (manifesto) — “Introduce a holistic and comprehensive National Food Strategy to ensure food security, tackle rising food prices, end food poverty and improve health and nutrition.”
  • Food insecurity falls disproportionately on low-income households and those with a disabled person, indicating the distributional dimension is concentrated at the bottom. — gov.uk (media) — “Vulnerable groups, including low-income households and those with a disabled person, face a disproportionately high risk of food insecurity.”
  • Meeting healthy eating guidelines costs the poorest fifth of households 50% of disposable income versus 11% for the richest fifth, showing a large inequality in food access. — pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov (government) — “Meeting the Government's recommended Eatwell Guide would cost the poorest fifth of UK households 50% of their disposable income, compared to 11% for the richest fifth.”
  • Children from the most deprived areas are twice as likely to be overweight or obese, showing dietary inequality tracks deprivation. — healthactionresearch.org.uk (media) — “Children from the most deprived areas are twice as likely to be overweight or obese compared to those from the least deprived areas.”
  • Independent review recommendations included a sugar and salt tax with revenue directed to support low-income families — a redistributive mechanism — but the government did not implement it. — york.ac.uk (academic) — “The government, however, did not implement this.”
  • Credible analysts broadly agree that the UK Government's actual food strategy fell short of the ambition needed, failing to adopt system-wide changes. — york.ac.uk (academic) — “credible analysts largely agree that the UK Government's subsequent Food Strategy falls short in its implementation and commitment to comprehensive, system-wide change, potentially undermining its stated goals of improvi…”
  • Experts argue the government's approach left the country more vulnerable to rising food prices through U-turns and lack of ambition. — reading.ac.uk (academic) — “Experts from the University of Reading and others argue that the government's "U-turns and lack of ambition" on food policy leave the nation "increasingly vulnerable to rising food prices."”

Biggest unknown: Whether the strategy would include redistributive mechanisms (such as a sugar/salt tax or expanded free school meals) that would actually shift resources toward lower-income households, or remain aspirational without binding instruments.

Our reading: The policy text commits only to introducing a strategy — it names desirable goals (ending food poverty, tackling food prices) but specifies no instrument, budget, statutory duty, or quantified target. Under the soft-verb rule, a commitment to introduce a strategy, without specifying its content, earns only candidacy, not a verdict of 'improves'. The distributional stakes are clear and large: low-income households spend a far greater share of income on food (50% of disposable income to meet dietary guidelines versus 11% for the richest), and food insecurity is heavily concentrated among the poorest. If the strategy included the redistributive mechanisms proposed in the independent review — a sugar/salt tax recycled to low-income families, extended free school meals — it would plausibly narrow the gap and score 'improves' on O14. But those mechanisms were not adopted in the actual government strategy that followed, and credible analysts (York, Sheffield, Sustain, Reading academics) agreed it fell well short. The crucial unknown is content: the same strategy label could contain anything from ambitious redistribution to a piecemeal aspiration document. Past evidence (E48, E46, E21) shows the gap between food-strategy rhetoric and delivered policy can be very large. No cited evidence supports a confident prediction that this iteration will include the specific instruments needed to move the inequality needle at population scale. The verdict is therefore too-uncertain, not because the goal points the wrong way — ending food poverty clearly would improve O14 — but because the policy text provides no committed mechanism, and the track record of prior food strategy efforts gives no reliable anchor for forecasting effect size or direction of redistribution.

Cost of living — Genuinely contested

n/a · low confidence

The policy promises to tackle rising food prices and end food poverty, but the stated goals are very broad and previous attempts at a National Food Strategy were widely criticised for failing to deliver meaningful change. Whether this version would actually cut food bills for ordinary households depends entirely on what concrete measures it includes.

The evidence

  • The policy commits to tackling rising food prices and ending food poverty as explicit goals. — libdems.org.uk (manifesto) — “tackle rising food prices, end food poverty and improve health and nutrition”
  • UK food prices rose 36% between January 2021 and April 2025, more than triple the rise in the preceding decade. — gov.uk (media) — “Between January 2021 and April 2025, UK food prices increased by 36%, more than triple the increase in the preceding decade (January 2011 to January 2021) which saw a 9.5% rise.”
  • Food price inflation peaked at 19.2% in 2023, the highest in 45 years. — gov.uk (media) — “In 2023, food price inflation peaked at 19.2%, the highest in 45 years.”
  • Meeting healthy eating guidelines would cost the poorest fifth of UK households 50% of their disposable income. — pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov (government) — “Meeting the Government's recommended Eatwell Guide would cost the poorest fifth of UK households 50% of their disposable income, compared to 11% for the richest fifth.”
  • Food bank use is significant: 4.2% of individuals lived in a household that accessed a food bank in 2024. — gov.uk (media) — “In 2024, 4.2% of individuals lived in a household that had accessed a food bank in the preceding year.”
  • A prior independent review recommended a sugar and salt reformulation tax as having the greatest potential to stimulate system change and reduce costs of poor diet. — york.ac.uk (academic) — “Experts criticize the government for failing to include the proposed sugar and salt tax, which the Dimbleby Review identified as having "the greatest potential to stimulate system change and break the 'junk food cycle'."”
  • The prior government Food Strategy was criticised as lacking ambition and failing to adopt key recommendations that would have tackled prices and poverty. — york.ac.uk (academic) — “credible analysts largely agree that the UK Government's subsequent Food Strategy falls short in its implementation and commitment to comprehensive, system-wide change, potentially undermining its stated goals of improvi…”
  • Experts argue that without the recommended fiscal and structural measures, the nation remains vulnerable to rising food prices. — reading.ac.uk (academic) — “Experts from the University of Reading and others argue that the government's "U-turns and lack of ambition" on food policy leave the nation "increasingly vulnerable to rising food prices."”
  • Critics from Sustain argue the strategy is too weak to address the cost-of-living crisis and rising food poverty. — sustainweb.org (media) — “Critics, such as Sustain, argue that the government's strategy is "shamefully weak" in the face of the cost-of-living crisis and rising food poverty, calling it "a feeble to do list, that may or may not get ticked."”
  • Food inflation is expected to rise further due to geopolitical factors and fertilizer costs. — youtube.com (media) — “food inflation is expected to rise further, partly due to the Iran war and fertilizer costs, noting that "food inflation is going to go up and it's one of the biggest political issues."”

Biggest unknown: Which specific mechanisms (e.g. a sugar/salt tax, extended free school meals, trade standards) are actually adopted — previous strategies omitted the measures experts said would have the most impact on prices and food poverty.

Our reading: The policy as stated is an aspiration rather than a defined set of measures. The need is acute: food prices rose 36% in four years, food bank use is at 4.2% of individuals, and healthy eating costs the poorest households 50% of their disposable income. The independent Dimbleby Review identified concrete tools — a sugar/salt reformulation tax, extended free school meals, trade standards — that experts across multiple universities and civil society organisations assessed as capable of reducing price pressure and food poverty. However, when a prior government published its own National Food Strategy in 2022, credible analysts broadly agreed it omitted these key instruments and delivered only piecemeal action. The word 'holistic and comprehensive' in the stated policy suggests ambition, but there is no evidence in the provided units that specific mechanisms are committed to. The verdict therefore hinges entirely on implementation: a version that adopts the Dimbleby recommendations would plausibly improve affordability for low-income households through reformulation incentives and direct food support; a version that again sidesteps the fiscal tools would leave ordinary households no better off despite the headline commitment. That range is too wide to resolve with the evidence available, making this genuinely too-uncertain.

Healthcare — Little effect

minor · low confidence

A National Food Strategy that genuinely improved diets could reduce NHS demand over the long term, but this policy is stated in entirely aspirational terms with no committed mechanism, budget, or target — so any healthcare benefit remains speculative. Expert analysis of previous real-world food strategies shows governments consistently fell short of the recommendations needed to drive meaningful change.

The evidence

  • The policy commits to improving health and nutrition as a goal, but specifies no instrument, budget, or enforceable target to achieve it. — libdems.org.uk (manifesto) — “Introduce a holistic and comprehensive National Food Strategy to ensure food security, tackle rising food prices, end food poverty and improve health and nutrition.”
  • Poor diets already contribute to approximately 64,000 deaths annually in England, and the government spends £18 billion each year treating obesity-related conditions — indicating a large potential healthcare gain if diet genuinely improved. — wcrf.org (media) — “Poor diets contribute to approximately 64,000 deaths annually in England, with the government spending £18 billion each year treating obesity-related conditions.”
  • Over 60% of the UK population lives with overweight or obesity, and one in four children leave primary school with overweight or obesity. — healthactionresearch.org.uk (media) — “Over 60% of the UK population is living with overweight or obesity, and one in four children leave primary school with overweight or obesity.”
  • Experts criticise previous food strategies for failing to include fiscal measures like a sugar and salt tax, which had the greatest evidence base for driving dietary reformulation and improving public health. — york.ac.uk (academic) — “Experts criticize the government for failing to include the proposed sugar and salt tax, which the Dimbleby Review identified as having "the greatest potential to stimulate system change and break the 'junk food cycle'."”
  • The Soft Drinks Industry Levy — a concrete fiscal instrument — demonstrated real-world impact by reducing sugar in drinks by 30g per household per week, illustrating what a binding mechanism can achieve versus aspirational strategies. — healthactionresearch.org.uk (media) — “the success of the Soft Drinks Industry Levy in reducing sugar in drinks by 30g per household per week”
  • Credible analysts broadly agree that aspirational food strategies without system-wide commitments fall short of improving public health and the environment. — york.ac.uk (academic) — “credible analysts largely agree that the UK Government's subsequent Food Strategy falls short in its implementation and commitment to comprehensive, system-wide change, potentially undermining its stated goals of improvi…”

Biggest unknown: Whether the strategy would include binding fiscal or regulatory instruments (like a sugar/salt tax) proven to shift dietary behaviour at population scale — without these, no healthcare gain is evidenced.

Our reading: The policy's stated goal of improving health and nutrition is directly relevant to O3: better diets reduce NHS demand, particularly for obesity-related conditions which cost £18 billion annually and contribute to 64,000 deaths. The baseline burden is large, so the potential upside is real. However, the policy text is entirely soft-verb and aspirational — 'introduce a holistic and comprehensive strategy' with no committed budget, statutory duty, quantified target, or named instrument. Under the soft-verb rule, this defaults to negligible unless evidence shows a credible delivery mechanism fires at scale. The evidence on previous real-world food strategies is damning: experts consistently found that governments adopted only piecemeal measures and omitted the fiscal tools (sugar/salt tax, extended free school meals) with the strongest evidence base. The Soft Drinks Industry Levy shows a concrete instrument can move behaviour, but this policy commits to no such instrument. Without a binding mechanism, the causal chain from 'strategy document' to 'reduced NHS waiting lists or improved health outcomes' is broken. I score direction as negligible rather than too-uncertain because the evidence leans clearly one way: aspirational food strategies without committed instruments have not delivered population-scale health improvements. A minor magnitude is assigned because the theoretical upside is non-trivial if instruments were added, but confidence is low given the gap between stated intent and evidenced delivery.
