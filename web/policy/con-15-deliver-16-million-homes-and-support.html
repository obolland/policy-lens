Deliver 1.6 Million Homes and Support First-Time Buyers

Conservative · what the evidence says

An independent, source-checked look at Conservative’s policy “Deliver 1.6 Million Homes and Support First-Time Buyers” — what it would actually do across the things that affect your life. Every claim below quotes the source behind it. How this works.

Affordable housing — Mixed picture

moderate · moderate confidence

This policy could add meaningful numbers of new homes over time, which should help supply — but demand-side measures like stamp duty relief and Help to Buy are likely to push prices up rather than down, and the 1.6 million target has not been achieved in decades. The net effect on affordability, especially for lower-income households, is genuinely mixed.

The evidence

  • The policy commits to delivering 1.6 million homes in England via nutrient neutrality abolition, brownfield fast-tracking, London density rises, and urban development corporations. — conservatives.com (manifesto) — “delivering 1.6 million homes in England by abolishing legacy EU 'nutrient neutrality' rules, fast-tracking homes on urban brownfield land, raising density in inner London, and creating urban development corporations”
  • Stamp Duty relief for first-time buyers up to £425,000 would be made permanent, and a new Help to Buy scheme launched. — conservatives.com (manifesto) — “make Stamp Duty permanent for first-time buyers up to £425,000 and launch a new Help to Buy scheme”
  • Delivering 300,000+ homes annually — what 1.6 million in five years implies — has not been achieved since the 1960s. — independent.co.uk (media) — “delivering 1.6 million homes in five years, averaging over 300,000 new homes annually, has not been achieved since the 1960s”
  • The planning system is under-resourced and without reform may block delivery regardless of targets. — lgcplus.com (media) — “questions its attainability without new policies to "unblock the planning system" and resource underfunded planning services”
  • Brownfield land in England could support up to 1.4 million new homes, with over half already having some planning permission. — cpre.org.uk (media) — “CPRE research suggests there is enough brownfield land in England to build 1.4 million new homes, with over half of these sites already having some form of planning permission”
  • Brownfield sites take longer to progress from permission to construction and to complete than greenfield sites. — savills.co.uk (media) — “brownfield sites take longer to progress from planning permission to construction (8 months for brownfield versus 6 months for greenfield) and also take longer to complete (23 months versus 18 months for greenfield)”
  • An excessive brownfield focus risks a slower delivery rate and higher attrition of consented homes. — savills.co.uk (media) — “An excessive focus on brownfield could lead to a slower rate of delivery and a higher "attrition rate" where homes granted consent fail to be built”
  • Developers' delays in building out existing planning permissions are a major blocker on housing numbers, more so than nutrient neutrality rules. — wcl.org.uk (media) — “developers' own delays in building out existing planning permissions have been a far bigger blocker on housing numbers”
  • The OBR concluded that previous stamp duty relief for first-time buyers fed through mainly to higher house prices and did not significantly improve affordability. — obr.uk (institutional) — “the majority of the value of such relief "fed through to higher house prices" and that it "has not had a significant impact in terms of improving the affordability" for first-time buyers”
  • The OBR warned that making stamp duty relief permanent would have a greater effect on prices than a temporary relief. — obr.uk (institutional) — “a permanent relief would have a greater effect on prices than a temporary one”
  • The previous Help to Buy scheme supported over 387,000 purchases but around 65,000 buyers could have afforded to buy without it, and it added roughly £8,250 to the average house price. — kirklees.gov.uk (government) — “the Help to Buy scheme added around £8,250 to the average house price (a 3.0% increase), arguing that by increasing mortgage accessibility, the scheme pushed up prices, worsening overall affordability for others”
  • Abolishing nutrient neutrality rules could unlock around 100,000 new homes immediately, though environmental groups warn it risks serious harm to freshwater ecosystems. — elmhurstenergy.co.uk (media) — “The policy aims to immediately unlock 100,000 new homes by removing 'nutrient neutrality' regulations, requiring developers to pay a one-off mitigation fee instead”
  • Urban development corporations have a track record of delivering homes — e.g. Milton Keynes averaged 3,100 units per year — but face criticism for democratic accountability. — ukonward.com (media) — “Milton Keynes Development Corporation delivered an average of 3,100 units per year between 1981 and 1997, at a time of declining national housebuilding”

Biggest unknown: Whether the supply measures actually deliver homes at scale — something not achieved since the 1960s — would determine whether new supply offsets the price-inflating effect of the demand-side subsidies.

Our reading: The policy has a genuine supply-side ambition: 1.6 million homes via planning reform, brownfield fast-tracking, density increases, and UDCs. The supply measures, if delivered, would improve affordability by increasing stock. UDCs have a real delivery track record, brownfield land capacity exists, and removing nutrient neutrality could unlock homes quickly. These are meaningful positives for O1. However, the delivery challenge is severe. The implied annual rate of 300,000+ homes has not been achieved since the 1960s. Brownfield sites are slower and costlier to develop than greenfield. Planning capacity remains a bottleneck. Developer build-out rates, not just permissions, are a key constraint. On the demand side, the stamp duty and Help to Buy measures work against the affordability goal. The OBR found that prior stamp duty relief mostly raised prices, not access, and warned that a permanent relief would inflate prices further. The previous Help to Buy scheme raised average prices by around £8,250 and benefited many who could have bought anyway. A new Help to Buy scheme risks the same dynamic unless supply meaningfully outpaces the demand stimulus — which, given delivery uncertainty, is not assured. The policy is therefore mixed: the supply measures are directionally correct for O1 and could moderately improve housing availability over the long term, but the demand-side subsidies are likely to inflate prices, especially if supply underdelivers. Lower-income and social-rent households benefit least from either Help to Buy or stamp duty relief, and social/affordable tenure is not specifically addressed. On balance, the supply intentions are positive but undermined by an ambitious target with a poor track record, and partially offset by demand measures that worsen price affordability.

Tax & the money you keep — Little effect

minor · moderate confidence

Making Stamp Duty relief permanent saves first-time buyers up to £425,000 in tax at purchase, but independent analysis finds the saving largely feeds into higher house prices rather than staying in buyers' pockets. The net money-in-pocket gain is therefore small at best.

The evidence

  • The policy makes Stamp Duty relief permanent for first-time buyers on homes up to £425,000. — conservatives.com (manifesto) — “make Stamp Duty permanent for first-time buyers up to £425,000”
  • Currently first-time buyers already pay no SDLT up to £425,000 under a relief that was due to expire in March 2025. — independent.co.uk (media) — “first-time buyers pay no Stamp Duty up to £425,000 and 5% on the portion from £425,001 to £625,000, a relief that was due to end in March 2025”
  • The OBR found that the majority of SDLT relief value feeds through to higher house prices rather than improving affordability for buyers. — obr.uk (institutional) — “the majority of the value of such relief "fed through to higher house prices" and that it "has not had a significant impact in terms of improving the affordability" for first-time buyers”
  • The OBR warned that making the relief permanent would have a greater price-inflation effect than a temporary one. — obr.uk (institutional) — “a permanent relief would have a greater effect on prices than a temporary one”
  • HMRC analysis of the 2017 SDLT relief found it led to a 1% increase in prices paid by first-time buyers in the £125,001–£300,000 band. — gov.uk (media) — “it also led to a 1% increase in prices paid by first-time buyers in the £125,001 to £300,000 band”

Biggest unknown: Whether a permanent, rather than temporary, SDLT relief drives a larger price capitalisation effect than past reliefs — the OBR explicitly flags this risk.

Our reading: O11 asks how much of what people earn they keep, judged by household tax burden and take-home pay. Making SDLT relief permanent does nominally remove a transaction tax for first-time buyers — on its face that is less tax paid. However, the OBR's institutional finding is that the majority of such relief's value capitalises into higher house prices rather than remaining in buyers' pockets. If prices rise to absorb the relief, the buyer's net financial position is little changed: they pay less SDLT but more for the property itself. The OBR further flags that permanence amplifies this price effect relative to a time-limited relief, which is precisely what this policy proposes. The HMRC baseline confirms a small but real price uplift even under the temporary version. Together, these evidence units indicate the nominal tax reduction does not translate into a material improvement in actual money kept. The policy does nothing to the tax burden on income, and its one direct tax instrument — SDLT — is expected to be largely swallowed by seller pricing. The verdict is therefore negligible: a real but immaterial improvement in nominal tax liability for a subset of buyers, offset by the well-evidenced price pass-through mechanism that erodes the net benefit.

Public finances & the next generation — Hurts

minor · moderate confidence

Making stamp duty relief permanent for first-time buyers and launching a new Help to Buy scheme both cost the Exchequer money without identified offsetting funding; the housebuilding measures are largely regulatory and carry minimal direct fiscal cost. The OBR has previously found SDLT relief mostly feeds into higher house prices, meaning the revenue loss may deliver little genuine economic return.

The evidence

  • The policy permanently abolishes Stamp Duty for first-time buyers on homes up to £425,000 and launches a new Help to Buy scheme. — conservatives.com (manifesto) — “make Stamp Duty permanent for first-time buyers up to £425,000 and launch a new Help to Buy scheme”
  • First-time buyers currently pay no Stamp Duty up to £425,000, a relief that was due to expire in March 2025 — making it permanent foregoes future revenue. — independent.co.uk (media) — “first-time buyers pay no Stamp Duty up to £425,000 and 5% on the portion from £425,001 to £625,000, a relief that was due to end in March 2025”
  • The OBR concluded that the majority of SDLT relief value 'fed through to higher house prices' and had not significantly improved affordability, with most beneficiaries likely to have purchased anyway. — obr.uk (institutional) — “the majority of the value of such relief "fed through to higher house prices" and that it "has not had a significant impact in terms of improving the affordability" for first-time buyers, with most beneficiaries likely t…”
  • The OBR further warned that a permanent relief would have a greater price-inflating effect than a temporary one, increasing the fiscal cost without proportionate benefit. — obr.uk (institutional) — “a permanent relief would have a greater effect on prices than a temporary one”
  • The previous Help to Buy equity-loan scheme supported 387,195 purchases but around 65,000 households (approximately three-fifths of a sample) could have afforded to buy without assistance, implying significant deadweight cost. — committees.parliament.uk (government) — “approximately three-fifths (around 65,000 households) of buyers using the scheme could have afforded to buy a home without government assistance”
  • The housebuilding measures — abolishing nutrient neutrality rules, fast-tracking brownfield, raising London density, and creating urban development corporations — are primarily regulatory and planning interventions rather than direct public spending commitments. — conservatives.com (manifesto) — “abolishing legacy EU 'nutrient neutrality' rules, fast-tracking homes on urban brownfield land, raising density in inner London, and creating urban development corporations”

Biggest unknown: The scale and structure of the new Help to Buy scheme is unspecified — if it replicates the equity-loan model it creates a significant contingent liability; if smaller, the fiscal impact is modest.

Our reading: The two direct fiscal instruments in this policy — permanent SDLT relief and a new Help to Buy scheme — both represent costs to the Exchequer with no identified funding source. The SDLT relief is an ongoing revenue foregone: the OBR has previously assessed that similar reliefs mostly inflate prices rather than improve affordability, meaning the Treasury loses revenue for limited real-economy return. Making it permanent, as the OBR notes, amplifies the price effect and thus the fiscal inefficiency. The new Help to Buy scheme is an unquantified spending commitment; the prior scheme created contingent liabilities via equity loans, and evaluations found substantial deadweight. Together these constitute unfunded demand-side subsidies — symmetrically problematic under O12's rubric regardless of political framing. The housebuilding supply-side measures (nutrient neutrality, brownfield fast-track, UDCs) are largely deregulatory or regulatory-reform in nature and carry minimal direct Exchequer cost; they do not offset the revenue/spending pressures from the demand-side instruments. The magnitude is judged minor rather than moderate because SDLT receipts from first-time buyers at this threshold are a relatively small share of total fiscal flows, and the Help to Buy scheme's scale is unspecified — if modest, aggregate impact on debt path is limited. Confidence is moderate because no OBR costing of this specific package exists in the provided evidence.

Prosperity & living standards — Mixed picture

moderate · low confidence

This policy could boost living standards and economic opportunity if a large-scale housebuilding programme is delivered, unlocking mobility, investment, and regeneration benefits. However, the 1.6 million target has not been reached in decades, and demand-side measures like stamp duty relief and Help to Buy risk inflating prices rather than improving real living standards.

The evidence

  • The policy commits to delivering 1.6 million homes by abolishing nutrient neutrality rules, fast-tracking brownfield development, raising London density, and creating urban development corporations, plus permanent stamp duty relief and a new Help to Buy scheme. — conservatives.com (manifesto) — “delivering 1.6 million homes in England by abolishing legacy EU 'nutrient neutrality' rules, fast-tracking homes on urban brownfield land, raising density in inner London, and creating urban development corporations”
  • Delivering 1.6 million homes over five years — averaging over 300,000 annually — has not been achieved since the 1960s. — independent.co.uk (media) — “delivering 1.6 million homes in five years, averaging over 300,000 new homes annually, has not been achieved since the 1960s”
  • Attaining the target faces serious challenges from underfunded planning services and the existing system's structural barriers. — lgcplus.com (media) — “questions its attainability without new policies to "unblock the planning system" and resource underfunded planning services”
  • Brownfield sites take longer than greenfield to move from planning permission to construction and completion. — savills.co.uk (media) — “brownfield sites take longer to progress from planning permission to construction (8 months for brownfield versus 6 months for greenfield) and also take longer to complete (23 months versus 18 months for greenfield)”
  • An excessive focus on brownfield could slow overall delivery and increase the rate at which consented homes are never built. — savills.co.uk (media) — “An excessive focus on brownfield could lead to a slower rate of delivery and a higher "attrition rate" where homes granted consent fail to be built”
  • Historical urban development corporations have delivered substantial homes and jobs — the London Docklands Development Corporation produced 24,000 homes and 120,000 jobs. — adamsmith.org (media) — “The London Docklands Development Corporation, for example, spurred 24,000 homes and 120,000 jobs”
  • UDCs can streamline planning and coordinate infrastructure, which could support economic opportunity and investment. — ukonward.com (media) — “UDCs can streamline planning, overcome land assembly barriers through compulsory purchase powers, and coordinate infrastructure development”
  • The OBR found that the majority of stamp duty relief value fed into higher house prices and had no significant affordability impact for first-time buyers. — obr.uk (institutional) — “the majority of the value of such relief "fed through to higher house prices" and that it "has not had a significant impact in terms of improving the affordability" for first-time buyers”
  • Making stamp duty relief permanent (rather than temporary) is projected by the OBR to have a greater price-inflating effect. — obr.uk (institutional) — “The OBR also suggested a permanent relief would have a greater effect on prices than a temporary one”
  • The previous Help to Buy scheme added around £8,250 to average house prices, worsening affordability for non-participants. — kirklees.gov.uk (government) — “Shelter research estimated that the Help to Buy scheme added around £8,250 to the average house price (a 3.0% increase), arguing that by increasing mortgage accessibility, the scheme pushed up prices”
  • Around 65,000 households using the previous Help to Buy could have bought without assistance, reducing its additionality. — committees.parliament.uk (government) — “approximately three-fifths (around 65,000 households) of buyers using the scheme could have afforded to buy a home without government assistance”
  • Brownfield regeneration could generate over 1 million new jobs and boost local economies if delivered. — johnfhuntregeneration.co.uk (media) — “Brownfield regeneration could also generate over 1 million new jobs and boost local economies”

Biggest unknown: Whether planning, construction-industry capacity, and brownfield delivery constraints allow anything close to 300,000+ homes per year to actually be built — without which most projected prosperity benefits do not materialise.

Our reading: For O13 — prosperity, living standards, and economic opportunity — this policy operates through two distinct channels with opposing evidence profiles. The supply-side channel (1.6m homes, brownfield fast-track, density, UDCs) has genuine long-term prosperity potential. More housing supply at scale would improve labour mobility, reduce housing cost drag on real living standards, and generate construction investment and regeneration gains. Historical UDC precedents (Docklands, Milton Keynes) show this can work. However, the delivery challenge is severe: 300,000+ homes per year has not been achieved in over sixty years, brownfield sites are slower and costlier to develop than greenfield, and the planning system's capacity constraints are widely flagged. Without near-full delivery, projected gains in mobility, investment, and living standards do not materialise. The demand-side channel (permanent SDLT relief, new Help to Buy) is more clearly problematic for O13. The OBR's assessment of prior SDLT relief found most of the value capitalised into prices rather than improving affordability, and warns that a permanent relief has a larger price-inflating effect than a temporary one. The previous Help to Buy scheme raised average prices by ~£8,250 and much of its support went to buyers who would have purchased anyway. These effects primarily redistribute wealth upward (to existing owners) rather than improving aggregate living standards or economic opportunity. They could however marginally support transaction volumes and construction-sector demand. The mixed verdict reflects a genuine divergence: the supply-side ambition, if even partially delivered, would be a moderate positive for O13's indicators (mobility, investment, job creation). The demand-side instruments are likely to be net-neutral or mildly negative for aggregate living standards by inflating asset prices. Confidence is low given the severe delivery uncertainty on the headline target.

Clean environment & nature — Hurts

moderate · moderate confidence

Abolishing nutrient neutrality rules risks exposing sensitive river ecosystems to more pollution from development; the brownfield and density elements are broadly positive for nature but don't offset the waterway risk.

The evidence

  • The policy abolishes legacy EU nutrient neutrality rules and replaces them with a one-off developer mitigation fee. — elmhurstenergy.co.uk (media) — “The policy aims to immediately unlock 100,000 new homes by removing 'nutrient neutrality' regulations, requiring developers to pay a one-off mitigation fee instead to ensure no net additional pollution.”
  • Environmental organisations including CIWEM, CPRE, RSPB and Devon Wildlife Trust regard nutrient neutrality rules as vital protection for waterways from development pollution. — ciwem.org (media) — “Organisations like CIWEM, CPRE, RSPB, and Devon Wildlife Trust contend that nutrient neutrality rules are vital for protecting waterways from pollution caused by development.”
  • Environmental groups argue abolition could be catastrophic for freshwater ecosystems and expose the UK's most sensitive rivers to more pollution. — wcl.org.uk (media) — “Environmental groups strongly oppose the abolition, arguing it could be "catastrophic for some of our most important freshwater spaces" and would "expose the UK's most sensitive river ecosystems to yet more pollution".”
  • The housebuilder lobby argues new homes make a negligible contribution — less than 1% — to nutrient levels in rivers. — lgcplus.com (media) — “The HBF argues that new homes make a "negligible contribution" to nutrient levels in rivers, less than 1%.”
  • Mitigation schemes were already proving effective at enabling housebuilding while offsetting pollution, so the 'housing moratorium' framing is disputed. — devonwildlifetrust.org (media) — “mitigation schemes were already proving effective in enabling housebuilding while offsetting pollution”
  • The House of Lords rejected similar amendments to the Habitats Regulations in 2023, indicating legal and political resistance to this approach. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “The House of Lords rejected similar proposed amendments to the Habitats Regulations in September 2023.”
  • The policy fast-tracks homes on urban brownfield land, which is widely supported as protecting green spaces by reducing pressure on the Green Belt. — gov.uk (media) — “Development on brownfield land is widely supported as it can regenerate communities, protect green spaces by reducing pressure on the Green Belt, and utilise existing infrastructure.”
  • Higher urban density is advocated by planners for environmental and social sustainability and reducing urban sprawl. — lse.ac.uk (academic) — “Urbanists and planners often advocate for increased density in cities for environmental and social sustainability, better utilisation of public transport, and reducing urban sprawl.”

Biggest unknown: Whether the proposed one-off mitigation fee would genuinely offset pollution loads in the most sensitive river catchments, or whether it simply monetises harm without preventing it.

Our reading: The policy's most direct environmental effect comes from abolishing nutrient neutrality rules. Independent environmental bodies — CIWEM, CPRE, RSPB, Devon Wildlife Trust — regard these rules as essential protection for sensitive river catchments. The counter-claim from the housebuilder lobby (HBF) that new homes contribute less than 1% of nutrient loads is from an advocacy source and should be weighted accordingly; it does not address cumulative or catchment-specific impacts. The proposed one-off mitigation fee is intended to substitute for the existing offsetting regime, but the evidence that mitigation schemes were already working to enable building while protecting waterways undermines the case that abolition is necessary for housing delivery. The House of Lords' rejection of comparable amendments reinforces that this is a contested legal and environmental question, not a settled one. On the positive side, the brownfield focus and urban densification elements are environmentally sound: concentrating development on previously used land reduces greenfield loss, and higher urban density reduces per-capita land take and supports public transport use. These are real, if modest, environmental gains. On balance, the removal of a statutory protection for waterways — applied across potentially 100,000 or more sites — is a material environmental downside that the brownfield and density elements do not offset. The effect is primarily long-term (river ecosystem health degrades gradually) and moderate in magnitude, given the genuine uncertainty about how effective the mitigation fee would be in practice. Confidence is moderate because the magnitude of the waterway impact depends on fee design and enforcement, which are not specified.
