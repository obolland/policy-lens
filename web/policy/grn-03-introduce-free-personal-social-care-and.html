Introduce free personal social care and reform the care workforce

Green · what the evidence says

An independent, source-checked look at Green’s policy “Introduce free personal social care and reform the care workforce” — what it would actually do across the things that affect your life. Every claim below quotes the source behind it. How this works.

Public finances & the next generation — Hurts

major · moderate confidence

The policy commits £20 billion a year to free personal care but does not identify a funding source, meaning it would likely add substantially to borrowing or require large tax rises. Independent estimates put the actual additional cost of free personal care at £5.5–7.9 billion, so the £20bn figure either overstates the cost or signals wider ambitions — either way, without a named revenue stream it poses a significant fiscal risk.

The evidence

  • The policy commits £20bn per year to fund free personal care. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “funded by £20bn per year”
  • Net expenditure on adult social care in England in 2023/24 was £26.9 billion. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “In 2023/24, net expenditure on adult social care in England was £26.9 billion (in 2024/25 prices)”
  • The Resolution Foundation and The King's Fund estimated the additional cost of free personal care at £5.5bn, rising to £7.9bn by 2030/31 — well below the £20bn stated. — reader.health.org.uk (institutional) — “The Resolution Foundation and The King's Fund estimated in 2020/21 that free personal care would cost an additional £5.5 billion, rising to £7.9 billion by 2030/31”
  • The OBR projects public spending on adult social care to rise from ~1.3% of national income in 2023–24 to ~2.2% by 2073–74 due to an ageing population, indicating the baseline cost trajectory is already rising before this policy. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) projects public spending on adult social care in the UK to rise from approximately 1.3% of national income in 2023–24 to around 2.2% by 2073–74, driven by an ageing population”
  • The Scottish model faced persistent affordability concerns, with local authorities struggling to fully fund it — twenty of Scotland's 32 local authorities needing an extra £70m in 2005 alone. — pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov (government) — “Twenty of Scotland's 32 local authorities needed an additional £70 million in 2005 to fully implement it”

Biggest unknown: Whether the £20bn is genuinely new unfunded borrowing, a reallocation from elsewhere, or a costings error — the policy text names no revenue source, and independent cost estimates are materially lower, leaving the true fiscal impact highly uncertain.

Our reading: The policy's central fiscal commitment is £20bn per year for free personal care, with no identified revenue source in the policy text. Against a current net social care spend of £26.9bn, this implies either a near-doubling of public social care expenditure or a very large reallocation. Independent estimates from the Resolution Foundation and King's Fund put the *additional* cost of free personal care at £5.5–7.9bn by 2030/31 — materially below the £20bn figure. The gap is unexplained and unaccounted for in the evidence provided. The OBR's baseline already projects social care costs rising significantly over the long run due to demographics, meaning the fiscal pressure compounds over time. The Scottish experience demonstrates real implementation underfunding risk: twenty of thirty-two Scottish councils needed extra funding in the policy's early years. Absent any stated funding mechanism — no tax, no bond, no reallocation — this policy as written is unfunded new spending on a large scale. Under the O12 criteria, unfunded consumption spending worsens the debt path; it neither finances productive investment that raises future capacity nor names a revenue offset. The workforce reforms (national pay, career structure) would add further recurrent costs to the public wage bill, also without stated funding. The direction is therefore 'worsens', and the magnitude is major given the headline £20bn annual commitment. Confidence is moderate rather than high because the true net cost is genuinely uncertain — the gap between the £20bn stated and the £5.5–7.9bn independent estimate could reflect a broader reform package, but that case is not made in the policy text.

Inequality & fair shares — Helps

moderate · moderate confidence

This policy narrows the inequality gap in two ways: it lifts pay and conditions for care workers who are among the lowest-paid in the economy, and it removes catastrophic care costs that currently wipe out the savings of ordinary families. A genuine caveat is that universal free personal care also protects wealthy people's assets, which blunts the redistributive effect.

The evidence

  • The policy commits £20bn per year to fund free personal care and establish a career structure for carers with national pay, terms, and conditions. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “Introduce free personal care, funded by £20bn per year, along the lines of the Scottish model, to ensure dignity in old age and for disabled people. Establish a career structure for carers with national pay, terms, and c…”
  • Care workers are among the lowest paid in the UK, with median hourly pay of £10.90 in April 2022 versus an economy-wide average of £14.47. — resolutionfoundation.org (institutional) — “Median hourly pay for frontline care workers was £10.90 in April 2022, compared to the economy-wide average of £14.47”
  • The pay premium care workers once held over other low-paid jobs has almost vanished. — resolutionfoundation.org (institutional) — “The "pay premium" care workers once held over other low-paid jobs has almost vanished”
  • Residential care workers and their families are nearly twice as likely to live in poverty as the average UK worker. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “Residential care workers and their families are nearly twice as likely to live in poverty as the average UK worker and are more likely to experience food insecurity or rely on Universal Credit”
  • Under the current system some older people face care costs exceeding £100,000, causing rapid depletion of savings and assets. — independentage.org (media) — “the high cost of care in England means individuals can rapidly deplete savings and assets, with some older people facing care costs exceeding £100,000 under the current system”
  • Unlawful underpayment of the minimum wage is potentially widespread for domiciliary care workers who are often not paid for travel time. — resolutionfoundation.org (institutional) — “The Resolution Foundation found that unlawful underpayment of the minimum wage is potentially widespread, particularly for domiciliary care workers who are often not paid for travel time between clients”
  • Some analysts have argued that universal free personal care acts as a subsidy for the better off, since it protects assets regardless of wealth. — pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov (government) — “Some Scottish Labour politicians initially viewed free personal care as a subsidy for the better off”
  • A career structure with national pay and conditions is projected to reduce the high annual care worker turnover rate of around 28% and make care work more attractive. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “A career structure with national pay, terms, and conditions would make care work more attractive, reducing the high annual turnover rate (estimated at around 28% in 2022/23)”

Biggest unknown: Whether the £20bn funding is sufficient and whether national pay for carers is set high enough to materially close the gap with economy-wide wages determines how much of the inequality effect actually materialises.

Our reading: The policy affects O14 through two distinct channels. First, the workforce reforms directly address one of the starkest pay gaps in the UK economy. Care workers earn roughly 75p for every £1 earned by the average worker, have lost their historic pay premium over other low-paid jobs, and suffer poverty rates nearly double the national average. National pay, terms, and conditions — if set meaningfully above current levels — would directly narrow income inequality for a large, predominantly female, low-paid workforce. The Resolution Foundation's evidence of widespread unlawful underpayment reinforces the scale of the current exploitation, making the reform's redistributive potential genuine. Second, free personal care redistributes financial risk away from ordinary families who currently face catastrophic costs that deplete savings built over a lifetime. This is progressive in practice even if universal in design: middle-income households who have modest assets to protect, and who currently self-fund until those assets are exhausted, gain the most in relative terms. The counter-argument — that universalism also shields the assets of the wealthy — has cited support (E15) and is a real distributional leakage; it blunts but does not reverse the progressive direction. The wealthy would still benefit, but the proportional relief to those who currently face ruinous means-tested costs is larger. The visa reform to end exploitation of overseas care workers also has a modest progressive effect, addressing a labour market where debt-bondage-style recruitment fees suppress worker bargaining power. Taken together, both channels point toward a narrowing of the income and wealth gap, with the workforce pay channel being the cleaner and more direct instrument. The main caveat is delivery: if funding proves insufficient (E12, E13 point to Scottish local authorities struggling), wages may not rise materially and the inequality effect weakens.

Healthcare — Helps

moderate · moderate confidence

Free personal care and a better-paid care workforce would ease pressure on NHS hospitals by reducing delayed discharges, and help more people get the social care they need — but the £20bn funding figure is well above independent cost estimates, and implementation challenges are significant.

The evidence

  • The policy commits to free personal care funded by £20bn per year, modelled on Scotland, plus a national career structure and pay framework for carers. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “Introduce free personal care, funded by £20bn per year, along the lines of the Scottish model, to ensure dignity in old age and for disabled people. Establish a career structure for carers with national pay, terms, and c…”
  • England's social care sector had a vacancy rate of 9.9% in 2022/23, far above the UK economy average of 3.5%. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “vacancy rate of 9.9% in 2022/23, significantly higher than the UK economy average of 3.5%”
  • Skills for Care reported 131,000 vacancies in 2024. — irwinmitchell.com (media) — “Skills for Care reported 131,000 vacancies in 2024”
  • Staff turnover in social care is estimated at around 28% in 2022/23. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “Estimated 28% staff turnover rate in 2022/23”
  • Median hourly pay for frontline care workers was £10.90 in April 2022, well below the economy-wide average of £14.47. — resolutionfoundation.org (institutional) — “Median hourly pay for frontline care workers was £10.90 in April 2022, compared to the economy-wide average of £14.47”
  • Free personal care in Scotland was associated with reduced bed-blocking in hospitals, freeing up NHS resources. — pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov (government) — “Analysis of the Scottish model indicated benefits such as reduced bed blocking in hospitals, freeing up NHS resources”
  • Since Scotland introduced free personal care, the number receiving it at home rose by 72% and care home numbers fell by nearly 6% between 2002 and 2018. — independentage.org (media) — “the number of people receiving free personal care at home increased by 72%, while the number in care homes decreased by almost 6% between 2002 and 2018”
  • Independent estimates put the cost of free personal care in England at £5.5bn rising to £7.9bn by 2030/31, well below the £20bn stated. — reader.health.org.uk (institutional) — “The Resolution Foundation and The King's Fund estimated in 2020/21 that free personal care would cost an additional £5.5 billion, rising to £7.9 billion by 2030/31”
  • Scotland's implementation faced persistent affordability concerns, with local authorities struggling to fully fund the policy. — pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov (government) — “the initial implementation in Scotland faced persistent concerns about long-term affordability, with local authorities struggling to fully fund the policy”
  • Increasing wages by 10% above the mean could reduce care worker leaver rates by approximately 3 percentage points. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “increasing wages by 10% above the mean could reduce leaver rates by approximately 3 percentage points”
  • A national career structure and pay would make care work more attractive and reduce the high turnover rate. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “A career structure with national pay, terms, and conditions would make care work more attractive, reducing the high annual turnover rate”
  • There is already a funding gap pressure in social care: the Association of Directors of Adult Social Services has identified a gap of over £1bn for social care to stand still. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “Association of Directors of Adult Social Services suggesting a "funding gap of over £1bn for adult social care to even stand still next year"”

Biggest unknown: Whether the £20bn funding commitment is sufficient and sustained, given that independent estimates put the cost of free personal care alone at £5.5–7.9bn and Scotland's local authorities struggled to fully fund the policy from the start.

Our reading: This policy has two distinct mechanisms that both bear on O3. First, free personal care removes financial barriers to accessing social care for older and disabled people. The Scottish precedent shows a 72% rise in people receiving care at home and a reduction in hospital bed-blocking — a direct gain for NHS capacity (O3's core indicator). With NHS waiting lists under sustained pressure, unblocking delayed discharges is a material lever. Second, the workforce reform — national pay, career structure, visa reforms — directly addresses the sector's severe staffing shortfall (131,000 vacancies, 28% turnover, median pay £4/hour below economy average). Evidence links higher wages to lower leaver rates, and chronic understaffing is a proximate cause of unmet care need and NHS overflow. The counterfactual — absent this policy — is continued workforce attrition and no universal care entitlement, with demand rising as the population ages (OBR projects social care spending needs doubling as a share of GDP by 2073–74). However, confidence is held to moderate rather than high because: the £20bn figure substantially exceeds independent cost estimates of £5.5–7.9bn, suggesting either significant funding slack (positive) or that the stated figure covers broader social care reform (ambiguous); Scotland's experience confirms implementation is hard and local funding gaps can undermine delivery; and the visa reform detail is vague given the complex and deteriorating policy environment around overseas care workers. The magnitude is moderate: the mechanism is evidenced and population-scale (social care interacts with NHS throughout England), but delivery risk is real and the time horizon is long-term given the scale of system change required.

Good work & fair pay — Helps

moderate · moderate confidence

This policy would establish national pay and career structures for care workers, who are among the lowest-paid workers in the UK — that should raise wages and reduce exploitation. The main risk is whether the £20bn funding is enough to actually deliver higher pay alongside free care, given the sector already faces a large funding gap.

The evidence

  • The policy would establish a career structure for carers with national pay, terms, and conditions. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “Establish a career structure for carers with national pay, terms, and conditions, and reform the working visa system to end exploitation of overseas care workers.”
  • Care workers are among the lowest paid in the UK, with median hourly pay of £10.90 in April 2022, well below the economy-wide average of £14.47. — resolutionfoundation.org (institutional) — “Median hourly pay for frontline care workers was £10.90 in April 2022, compared to the economy-wide average of £14.47”
  • The pay premium care workers once held over other low-paid jobs has nearly disappeared. — resolutionfoundation.org (institutional) — “The "pay premium" care workers once held over other low-paid jobs has almost vanished”
  • Residential care workers are nearly twice as likely to live in poverty as the average UK worker. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “Residential care workers and their families are nearly twice as likely to live in poverty as the average UK worker and are more likely to experience food insecurity or rely on Universal Credit”
  • The sector had an estimated 28% staff turnover rate in 2022/23, far above economy-wide norms. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “Estimated 28% staff turnover rate in 2022/23”
  • The vacancy rate in adult social care was 9.9% in 2022/23, with 131,000 vacancies in 2024, far above the UK economy average of 3.5%. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “a vacancy rate of 9.9% in 2022/23, significantly higher than the UK economy average of 3.5%”
  • Unlawful underpayment of minimum wage is potentially widespread among domiciliary care workers, who are often not paid for travel time between clients. — resolutionfoundation.org (institutional) — “unlawful underpayment of the minimum wage is potentially widespread, particularly for domiciliary care workers who are often not paid for travel time between clients”
  • A national career structure with pay standardisation could reduce the high turnover rate and make care work more attractive. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “A career structure with national pay, terms, and conditions would make care work more attractive, reducing the high annual turnover rate (estimated at around 28% in 2022/23)”
  • Increasing care worker pay could reduce leaver rates; a 10% wage rise above the mean is estimated to cut leaver rates by about 3 percentage points. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “increasing wages by 10% above the mean could reduce leaver rates by approximately 3 percentage points”
  • The sector already faces a funding gap of over £1bn just to stand still, with rising NLW and NIC costs consuming additional government funding. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “there's concern it will be absorbed by these rising costs, with the Association of Directors of Adult Social Services suggesting a "funding gap of over £1bn for adult social care to even stand still next year"”

Biggest unknown: Whether the £20bn funding commitment is sufficient to both provide free personal care and fund meaningfully higher national pay for carers, given existing cost pressures in the sector.

Our reading: The baseline for care workers is poor: median pay well below the economy average, near-poverty rates, a vanishing pay premium, 28% annual turnover, and widespread unlawful underpayment. These are exactly the indicators O4 tracks. A national pay structure with defined career progression directly targets the root causes: low wages making care work unattractive relative to other low-paid sectors, and lack of career pathway driving churn. Evidence from the Scottish model shows that free personal care increased home-care uptake substantially, which would expand the workforce's workload — making the simultaneous workforce reform critical rather than optional. The projected benefits (lower turnover, better recruitment, reduced exploitation of overseas workers) are supported by credible evidence linking pay increases to retention. However, the magnitude is held to 'moderate' rather than 'major' for two reasons. First, the sector already faces a funding gap exceeding £1bn under current cost pressures; it is not certain that the £20bn primarily funds workforce improvements rather than being consumed by the cost of providing free care itself. Second, ongoing government visa restrictions on care workers cut against the policy's stated aim of ending overseas worker exploitation, creating a countervailing pressure on workforce supply. The direction is nonetheless 'improves' because the stated national pay and career structure commitment, if funded, would materially benefit one of the most underpaid and exploited workforces in the UK economy — and the evidence clearly supports the link between higher pay, lower turnover, and better job quality in this sector.

Security in later life — Helps

major · moderate confidence

This policy would remove care costs for millions of older and disabled people, protecting them from catastrophic bills and improving dignity — but its £20bn funding claim is well above independent estimates, and delivery depends on solving deep workforce shortages.

The evidence

  • The policy promises free personal care funded by £20bn per year, modelled on Scotland, to ensure dignity in old age and for disabled people. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “Introduce free personal care, funded by £20bn per year, along the lines of the Scottish model, to ensure dignity in old age and for disabled people.”
  • The policy commits to a national career structure for carers with national pay, terms and conditions, and reform of the working visa system to end exploitation of overseas care workers. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “Establish a career structure for carers with national pay, terms, and conditions, and reform the working visa system to end exploitation of overseas care workers.”
  • Personal care covers assistance with hygiene, continence, mobility, food and diet, dressing, and similar tasks — but not hotel costs such as accommodation or food in residential settings. — independentage.org (media) — “this generally does not cover "hotel costs" (accommodation, food, building upkeep) in residential care, nor other forms of support like cleaning or community activities”
  • Under the current system, older people can face care costs exceeding £100,000, rapidly depleting savings and assets. — independentage.org (media) — “the high cost of care in England means individuals can rapidly deplete savings and assets, with some older people facing care costs exceeding £100,000 under the current system”
  • The social care sector in England has a vacancy rate of 9.9% in 2022/23, far above the UK economy average of 3.5%. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “social care sector in England faces severe workforce shortages, with a vacancy rate of 9.9% in 2022/23, significantly higher than the UK economy average of 3.5%”
  • Frontline care worker median hourly pay was £10.90 in April 2022, well below the economy-wide average of £14.47. — resolutionfoundation.org (institutional) — “Median hourly pay for frontline care workers was £10.90 in April 2022, compared to the economy-wide average of £14.47”
  • Annual staff turnover in adult social care is estimated at around 28% in 2022/23. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “Estimated 28% staff turnover rate in 2022/23”
  • The Resolution Foundation and The King's Fund estimated free personal care would cost an additional £5.5bn, rising to £7.9bn by 2030/31 — well below the £20bn headline figure stated. — reader.health.org.uk (institutional) — “The Resolution Foundation and The King's Fund estimated in 2020/21 that free personal care would cost an additional £5.5 billion, rising to £7.9 billion by 2030/31”
  • Scotland's experience suggests free personal care increases the number of people receiving care at home by 72% while reducing care home numbers, but local authorities struggled with affordability — 20 of 32 councils needed an extra £70m in 2005 to implement it. — pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov (government) — “Twenty of Scotland's 32 local authorities needed an additional £70 million in 2005 to fully implement it”
  • A national pay and career structure could reduce turnover: research suggests a 10% wage increase above the mean could cut leaver rates by approximately 3 percentage points. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “increasing wages by 10% above the mean could reduce leaver rates by approximately 3 percentage points”
  • Free personal care in Scotland was associated with reduced hospital bed-blocking and freed NHS resources. — pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov (government) — “Analysis of the Scottish model indicated benefits such as reduced bed blocking in hospitals, freeing up NHS resources”
  • Current adult social care net expenditure in England is already £26.9bn in 2023/24, and a funding gap of over £1bn has been flagged just to stand still. — commonslibrary.parliament.uk (government) — “the Association of Directors of Adult Social Services suggesting a "funding gap of over £1bn for adult social care to even stand still next year"”

Biggest unknown: Whether £20bn is genuinely sufficient and sustainably funded, given independent estimates put free personal care alone at £5.5–7.9bn extra and existing cost pressures already exceed current budgets.

Our reading: This policy directly targets the core indicators of O8: it removes the catastrophic financial risk older and disabled people face from personal care costs, a system currently leaving individuals depleting over £100,000 in savings. The Scottish model evidence shows free personal care increases home-based care access substantially and removes the unfairness of means-testing conditions like dementia while leaving medical treatment free. These are concrete improvements to dignity and security in later life. The workforce reforms also address a genuine structural problem — 9.9% vacancy rates, 28% turnover, and median pay far below the economy average all threaten the quality and continuity of care older people receive. A national career structure with proper pay would, on the evidence, reduce turnover and improve care quality. However, the £20bn funding figure is hard to reconcile with independent estimates of £5.5–7.9bn for free personal care alone, and the sector already faces a structural funding gap. Scotland's own implementation saw local authorities underfunded from the start. This creates real risk that the policy is under-resourced in practice, limiting its real-world impact. The distinction between personal care and hotel costs also means residential care users would still face substantial bills. Overall, the direction is clearly positive for O8 — removing financial catastrophe risk, improving workforce stability, and extending dignity — but the scale of improvement depends heavily on whether funding is genuinely adequate and sustainable. Confidence is moderate rather than high because the fiscal arithmetic is contested and delivery risk is substantial.

Immigration & border control — Direction unclear

We don’t call this better or worse — that’s your call; we only show which way the policy moves it.

n/a · low confidence

The policy says it would reform the visa system for overseas care workers to stop exploitation, but does not say whether more or fewer overseas workers would be admitted. It is not clear whether this would raise or lower net migration.

The evidence

  • The policy commits to reforming the working visa system to end exploitation of overseas care workers, alongside establishing a national career structure for carers. — greenparty.org.uk (manifesto) — “reform the working visa system to end exploitation of overseas care workers”
  • Trade unions warn that current visa restrictions are likely to worsen staffing shortages. — nursinginpractice.com (media) — “likely to worsen staffing shortages and put vital services at risk”
  • Improving pay and career structure domestically could reduce reliance on overseas recruitment, with a 10% wage rise estimated to reduce leaver rates by around 3 percentage points. — vertexaisearch.cloud.google.com (media) — “increasing wages by 10% above the mean could reduce leaver rates by approximately 3 percentage points”

Biggest unknown: Whether 'reform the working visa system' means opening new or expanded visa routes, maintaining existing ones with better protections, or restricting them further — the policy text does not specify the direction of change to entry rules.

Our reading: The only immigration-relevant element of this policy is the commitment to 'reform the working visa system to end exploitation of overseas care workers.' The text does not state whether this means expanding, restricting, or simply improving conditions within existing visa routes. The current policy backdrop (E31) is one of tightening — the care worker visa route is being closed to new overseas applicants. If this policy reverses that, it would move toward more open; if it merely adds worker protections within a restricted system, net migration effect is negligible. Because the direction of the visa reform is unspecified, no confident directional verdict can be reached.
